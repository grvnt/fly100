# Step: high C → D

The brief requires this step (and the two above/below it in difficulty) to carry much stricter requirements than the earlier jumps. The research supports that direction clearly, even though precise numbers remain sparse.

## Hours / experience

- [Flybubble — Paragliders: Which Class?](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) (industry): names this tier "Advanced (EN D)" and asks for 100+ *incident-free* thermic hours *per year*, ongoing — i.e. framed as a sustained annual-currency requirement, not a one-off cumulative total.
- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry, same retailer, different article): converges reasonably well here — also names 100+ hours per year for EN-D, alongside "natural flying instincts."
- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): gives a cumulative figure instead — 200+ hours of thermic flying cumulative, *plus* 100+ hours per year ongoing — effectively combining both a total and a currency requirement, which is a useful structure for the app to borrow given the brief already separates "total" from "recent" hours as inputs.
- [xcmag — How to Fly Advanced Paragliders, featuring Seb Ospina](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/) (industry/expert): doesn't give a single number, but repeats 100+ hours/year as a baseline and frames the real test as whether the pilot is already flying at the edge of their current (high-C) wing's capability, across a range of real conditions, not just in theory.

**Consensus read:** "100+ hours per year, ongoing" is the most consistently repeated figure in this entire research pass — three independent sources (two from Flybubble, one from Seb Ospina via xcmag) converge on it almost exactly, making it one of the better-supported numbers in the whole ladder, even though none of these are official bodies.

## Skills

- [Cross Country Magazine — First Time Two-Line](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) (industry/expert) frames any EN-D wing, 2-liner or not, as requiring the pilot to already be "well versed in SIV" — see SIV section.
- [Ozone — Enzo positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/ozone-enzo) (manufacturer via retailer): the Enzo (EN-D, open-class competition wing) is explicitly described as suitable only for "PWC level" pilots with "a very high standard of canopy control," current and in-depth SIV knowledge, and the specific skill of flying a 2-liner without it collapsing — illustrating how much further manufacturers push the bar at the very top of EN-D specifically, versus a more XC-oriented EN-D wing.
- [Ozone — Zeno/LM6 positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/ozone-zeno) (manufacturer via retailer): Zeno is described as needing a pilot already "deeply familiar" with the 2-liner class, i.e. explicitly not a wing to learn 2-liner skills on.
- Speed bar (added Oct 2026): [a class-by-class speedbar ladder mirrored on GitHub](https://github.com/twpayne/paragliding-articles/blob/master/articles/speed-bar-when-to-use.md) (provenance uncertain, see `sources.md`) places EN-D/competition wings at the top of its scale — bar use treated as a routine, constantly-used control, "like changing gears," rather than an occasional tool. [XC Mag — Active flying](https://xcmag.com/other/active-flying/) (industry) is explicit that this class of wing needs genuinely active pilot input to recover from a collapse, unlike intermediate wings which tend to self-recover — meaning the near-constant bar use expected at this step comes with correspondingly less margin for a slow or hesitant reaction.
- Ground handling (added Oct 2026): [paraglidingforum.com — Ground Training/Handling with an old EN-D wing](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=109843) (community) is the only material found addressing ground handling at the EN-D level specifically, and it's a caution rather than a skill checklist: experienced posters describe EN-D wings as "a nightmare to ground handle," intimidating even for already-competent pilots, and explicitly advise against using an old or unfamiliar D wing as a ground-training tool. This doesn't give the app a positive skill bar to test against, but it's a genuine, if thin, data point that ground-handling difficulty itself scales sharply at this step.

## Conditions flown

- The repeated "100+ hours/year" framing implicitly demands flying across a full season's range of conditions, not just occasional good-weather days — currency itself is treated as a safety requirement at this step, more explicitly than at any earlier one.

## Collapse handling

- Same collapse-behaviour caveats as the 2-liner step apply wherever the specific D-class wing is a 2-liner (most are, at the top end) — block-style collapses, higher cravat risk, need for precise and fast pilot input (this is literally the EN-D certification language: "demanding... potentially violent reactions... requires precise pilot actions").
- No source gave a specific collapse-frequency number unique to this step; the qualitative bar ("precise, immediate input required") is treated as the standard rather than a count.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- Seb Ospina's interview (via xcmag) is the most pointed psychological source in the ladder here: he explicitly describes his own early jump to high-end wings (age 17-18) as something that set his progression *back* rather than forward, directly warning against ambition-driven upgrades at this level.
- Why they want to upgrade (added Oct 2026): [Passion Paragliding](https://www.passionparagliding.com/how-to-choose-a-paraglider) (industry) reinforces Ospina's point from a different angle, naming peer pressure and identity/ego ("to validate your identity as a real pilot") as common, specific drivers of premature upgrades — directly relevant at this step given how much higher the stakes are than at high-B→low-C, where the same source first raises this concern. [XC Mag — The Head Game](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/) (industry/expert) adds a more measured counterpoint worth weighing against Ospina's cautionary framing: ambition itself isn't treated as the problem by Guschlbauer (a multi-time X-Alps podium finisher) — the risk is ambition that outpaces honest preparation, not the desire for more performance as such. See `disagreements.md` for this tension.
- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert) doesn't address EN-D specifically, but his general compounding-risk framework applies with extra force at this step: given how many of this step's other risk factors are already elevated (collapse violence, currency demands, SIV expectations), his recommendation to compensate elsewhere — conservative sites, less aggressive flying, more pre-flight study — is arguably most relevant of all at this point in the ladder, even though he never names EN-D directly.

## Red flags

- Advancing to EN-D off the back of raw enthusiasm or performance envy, without the 100+ hours/year currency or SIV fluency described above, is the consistent red-flag pattern.
- Ospina's own story is explicitly offered as a cautionary example, not an aspirational one.

## SIV

- This is the step where SIV is most consistently described as effectively necessary by industry and expert sources (Flybubble, xcmag/Malin Lobb, Ozone's own positioning of the Enzo). Even so, per the brief, the app should keep SIV as optional context rather than a hard gate — partly because no *official* source makes it a formal requirement even here (see `disagreements.md`), and partly because the brief is explicit that SIV's value is contested and shouldn't be a major scored factor regardless of step.

## Source-quality note

Hours-currency consensus (100+/year) is unusually strong for this step. Skills and psychological material come more from expert/manufacturer voices than from independent corroboration, which is appropriate given how few pilots operate at this level — there simply isn't a large independent literature to draw on. Treat this step's qualitative guidance (SIV fluency, currency, "don't rush it" framing) as reliable; treat any single specific number as indicative rather than definitive.
