# Step: mid B → high B

Better evidenced than the previous step, largely because manufacturers are explicit that their "high-B" models are a deliberate step up in demands, and because this is where most sources locate the real transition into serious XC flying.

## Hours / experience

- [Cross Country Magazine — When to move up a class of paraglider](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class) (industry): roughly 50 hours of thermic flying (or roughly 100 hours of ridge soaring) as the point where a pilot should consider a high-B, often triggered by the pilot feeling frustrated with sluggish handling and lack of feedback on their current wing.
- [Flybubble — Paragliders: Which Class?](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) (industry): names this tier "XC (high EN-B)" and asks for at least 50 hours of incident-free thermic flying.
- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): also lands on 50+ hours of thermic flying as the threshold for high-B, matching Flybubble and XC Mag reasonably closely — this is one of the better-corroborated hours figures in the whole ladder.
- [paragliding24.ch](https://paragliding24.ch/en/blogs/blog/en-b-gleitschirm-vergleich-low-vs-mid-vs-high-en-b) (industry): by contrast frames high-B as 50-80 *annual* hours, a different (and less comparable) framing from the cumulative-hours figures above — see `disagreements.md`.

**Consensus read:** three independent industry sources converge on roughly 50 cumulative hours of thermic flying as the practical marker for moving into high-B. This is one of the better-agreed figures anywhere in this research, though it's worth noting all three are industry/retailer sources rather than official or deeply independent.

## Skills

- [Advance — Iota positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/blog/advance-iota-reviews) (manufacturer via retailer): explicitly calls the step up to a high-B wing "a clear step up in demands on the pilot," specifically around doing the right things in turbulence, and cautions that it's "probably not ideal" as a pilot's very first high-B unless they're naturally talented or flying very current.
- [Gin — Explorer positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/blog/gin-explorer-paraglider-reviews) (manufacturer via retailer): matches this almost exactly — the Explorer is reserved for pilots who are naturally talented or very current, and is described as not ideal as someone's first high-B.
- [Nova — Mentor / Sector positioning](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/mentor-6/) (manufacturer): the Mentor (their benchmark high-B) is explicitly "not suitable for beginners and occasional pilots."
- [xcmag — How to Fly Advanced Paragliders, quoting Seb Ospina](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/) (industry/expert): frames genuine readiness less as a number and more as a behavioural signal — consistently maxing out what the current wing can give you, and reading air well enough that a better-performing wing's extra feedback is actually useful rather than wasted on you.

## Conditions flown

- Convergent theme across manufacturer positioning (Advance, Gin, Nova) and Flybubble: high-B is explicitly for pilots moving into proper cross-country flying and more turbulent thermic air, not just bigger ridge days. Multiple sources frame this as the step where the wing starts rewarding (and requiring) active piloting in rough air rather than just tolerating it.

## Collapse handling

- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry) recommends regular proactive practice of wingovers, spirals and asymmetric collapses around this stage of flying, not just theoretical knowledge.
- No source gave a specific collapse-frequency number for this exact step (that appears later, at the C/D steps).

## Psychological / mindset factors

- The manufacturer-via-retailer pattern (Advance, Gin) explicitly warns against buying a high-B *because* of ambition rather than current ability — i.e., "naturally talented or very current" is being used as a polite way of saying "not everyone who wants this wing should have it yet."
- Seb Ospina's framing (via xcmag) is the clearest psychological-readiness marker in this step: real readiness looks like having already been limited by your current wing in practice (maxed it out, crossed terrain others on higher-class wings navigate easily), not just wanting more performance in the abstract.

## Red flags

- Buying a high-B as an "aspirational" first upgrade rather than because you've demonstrably outgrown your current wing is flagged, in different words, by Advance, Gin, and Seb Ospina's interview.

## SIV

Not treated as a gate for this step by any source we found — it's notably absent here even in sources (Flybubble) that start requiring it at the next step up.

## Source-quality note

Reasonably well evidenced: three independent industry sources agree on ~50 hours, and three manufacturer-via-retailer sources independently agree on the "not for beginners/occasional pilots, needs genuine currency" framing. This is one of the stronger-consensus steps in the ladder.
