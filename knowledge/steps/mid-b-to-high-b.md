# Step: mid B → high B

Better evidenced than the previous step, largely because manufacturers are explicit that their "high-B" models are a deliberate step up in demands, and because this is where most sources locate the real transition into serious XC flying.

## Hours / experience

- [Greg Hamerton, via Cross Country Magazine — When to move up a class of paraglider](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) (expert — see `sources.md`): roughly 50 hours of thermic flying (or roughly 100 hours of ridge soaring) as the point where a pilot should consider a high-B, often triggered by the pilot feeling frustrated with sluggish handling and lack of feedback on their current wing.
- [Flybubble — Paragliders: Which Class?](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) (industry): names this tier "XC (high EN-B)" and asks for at least 50 hours of incident-free thermic flying.
- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): also lands on 50+ hours of thermic flying as the threshold for high-B, matching Flybubble and XC Mag reasonably closely — this is one of the better-corroborated hours figures in the whole ladder.
- [paragliding24.ch](https://paragliding24.ch/en/blogs/blog/en-b-gleitschirm-vergleich-low-vs-mid-vs-high-en-b) (industry): by contrast frames high-B as 50-80 *annual* hours, a different (and less comparable) framing from the cumulative-hours figures above — see `disagreements.md`.

**Consensus read:** three sources converge on roughly 50 cumulative hours of thermic flying as the practical marker for moving into high-B. **Independence caveat added Oct 2026:** the Cross Country Magazine piece is written by Greg Hamerton, who was Flybubble's long-time content lead/instructor before starting his own platform. That doesn't make the figure wrong, but it means this isn't really three fully independent sources converging — it's closer to two (a Hamerton/Flybubble house view, plus 406 Paragliding as the one genuinely separate voice). Treat the agreement as real but slightly less broad than it first appears. See `disagreements.md` point 6.

## Skills

- [Advance — Iota positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/blog/advance-iota-reviews) (manufacturer via retailer): explicitly calls the step up to a high-B wing "a clear step up in demands on the pilot," specifically around doing the right things in turbulence, and cautions that it's "probably not ideal" as a pilot's very first high-B unless they're naturally talented or flying very current.
- [Gin — Explorer positioning, via Flybubble](https://flybubble.com/blog/gin-explorer-paraglider-reviews) (manufacturer via retailer): matches this almost exactly — the Explorer is reserved for pilots who are naturally talented or very current, and is described as not ideal as someone's first high-B.
- [Nova — Mentor / Sector positioning](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/mentor-6/) (manufacturer): the Mentor (their benchmark high-B) is explicitly "not suitable for beginners and occasional pilots."
- [xcmag — How to Fly Advanced Paragliders, quoting Seb Ospina](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/) (industry/expert): frames genuine readiness less as a number and more as a behavioural signal — consistently maxing out what the current wing can give you, and reading air well enough that a better-performing wing's extra feedback is actually useful rather than wasted on you.
- Speed bar (added Oct 2026): [XC Mag — speed bar tips](https://xcmag.com/news/speed-bar-tips/) (industry) draws a skill contrast directly relevant to this step — a pilot who eases onto the bar while actively monitoring pitch reaches full acceleration with meaningfully more height in hand than one who stomps it on; it also describes skilled pilots continuously "trimming" bar pressure to hold the wing overhead rather than locking it fully engaged, which is a reasonable marker of the active-piloting-on-bar skill this step's sources (Advance, Gin, Nova, Flybubble) expect more generally. [Flybubble — Speed To Fly Basics](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/speed-to-fly-basics) (industry) adds that higher-performance wing categories reward bar use at lower headwind/sink thresholds than beginner-class wings — consistent with this step being where speed bar starts becoming a genuinely useful XC tool rather than an occasional extra.
- Ground handling (added Oct 2026): no source gave a ground-handling test specific to this exact step. [Flybubble — Improve your ground handling](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/improve-your-ground-handling) (industry) is the closest relevant material: it frames ground handling as directly teaching a pilot to recognise, by feel, what a wing looks and feels like just before it collapses — a skill this step's sources already expect in the air (proactive wingover/spiral/asymmetric practice, per Flybubble's Reducing Risk article below), so it's reasonable to treat ground-handling fluency as a supporting, non-gating signal here even though no source states this explicitly for the mid-B/high-B line. Worth noting the limit of official coverage here too: we checked [BHPA's own Pilot Rating Scheme page](https://www.bhpa.co.uk/safety/prs/) (official) specifically for whether ground handling is named as part of its Pilot or Advanced Pilot rating descriptions — it isn't; both are described only in general, qualitative terms ("well-rounded skills," "total master of his or her craft") with no specific mention of ground handling at all. So the gap here isn't just thin industry coverage — it extends to the one official UK pilot-rating source we were able to check directly.
- [Episode 37 — Groundhandlix, Urs Aschmann](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0037-groundhandlix-urs-aschmann/de/) (expert, Podz-Glidz podcast, German transcript, paraphrased, added Oct 2026) directly corroborates Flybubble's "recognise a collapse by feel" framing above with a named technique: a circling exercise around the wing's centre using the rear risers, which deliberately rolls one tip out of the wind window before an inbound gust can collapse it — explicitly presented as a skill with headroom well past the beginner stage, not something finished by this point.
- Active piloting / hand position by flight phase (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert — DHV instructor and steering-technique spokesperson, German transcript, paraphrased) gives the most granular skill description found anywhere in this knowledge base for this stage: static, "hands lightly on brakes at all times" flying is explicitly discouraged as a false sense of security; instead hand position should shift with flight phase — hands fully up on calm glides, a light "contact position" in mild turbulence to sense pressure changes, dropping to mid-brake-travel for active correction in thermic lift. He also describes rear-riser ("BC-line") steering becoming genuinely useful at this level for speed flying, but flags the transition back to brakes in turbulence as a danger point if rushed — an abrupt switch can pitch the wing down and trigger a collapse rather than prevent one. He states, as his own comparative judgement rather than a cited study, that a pilot with 100+ hours on a low-B is in a safer position moving onto a high-C than a pilot with only ~20 hours who jumps straight there — a new, single-source data point for the hours-thresholds disagreement; see `disagreements.md`.

## Conditions flown

- Convergent theme across manufacturer positioning (Advance, Gin, Nova) and Flybubble: high-B is explicitly for pilots moving into proper cross-country flying and more turbulent thermic air, not just bigger ridge days. Multiple sources frame this as the step where the wing starts rewarding (and requiring) active piloting in rough air rather than just tolerating it.

## Collapse handling

- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry) recommends regular proactive practice of wingovers, spirals and asymmetric collapses around this stage of flying, not just theoretical knowledge.
- No source gave a specific collapse-frequency number for this exact step (that appears later, at the C/D steps).
- A genuinely new point for this knowledge base, added Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert) argues that from EN-C upward, wings become so progressively resistant to collapsing that realistic collapse-recovery training becomes very difficult to simulate on the wing itself during an SIV course — so the actual muscle-memory training for collapse recovery has to happen on lower-class wings (i.e. around this step and the one below, not deferred to C/D). This is a mechanism-based argument for *earlier* collapse-response training than most of this knowledge base's other sources imply — see the update to `disagreements.md` point 8.
- He also shares a personal accident case directly relevant to collapse-handling conditions awareness: a frontal collapse in otherwise benign-feeling air while gliding low over a sun-heated industrial roof, which he uses to generalise a caution about heated/thermogenic ground surfaces (metal roofs, asphalt, sheltered sun-traps) producing sudden, violent turbulence near the ground even on days that don't look rough from altitude.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- The manufacturer-via-retailer pattern (Advance, Gin) explicitly warns against buying a high-B *because* of ambition rather than current ability — i.e., "naturally talented or very current" is being used as a polite way of saying "not everyone who wants this wing should have it yet."
- Seb Ospina's framing (via xcmag) is the clearest psychological-readiness marker in this step: real readiness looks like having already been limited by your current wing in practice (maxed it out, crossed terrain others on higher-class wings navigate easily), not just wanting more performance in the abstract.

- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk, hosted on old.fai.org/CIVL safety pages](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert): not written about this step specifically, but gives a general principle that applies squarely here — when a pilot takes on new or upgraded gear, risk rises simply from unfamiliarity, so he recommends deliberately compensating elsewhere for a period: choosing more conservative sites, flying less aggressively, and front-loading study of the new wing's specific handling. This is a genuinely new, practical point not found in any other source in this knowledge base, and fits naturally as a suggestion the app could surface at every upgrade step, not just this one.
- Why they want to upgrade (added Oct 2026): [Greg Hamerton, via Cross Country Magazine](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) (expert) opens his own upgrade guidance with exactly this question — "why do you want to upgrade?" — answers it plainly ("to get more performance"), then pivots immediately to "are you ready?", explicitly sequencing motive before readiness. This matches, and gives direct textual confirmation of, the behavioural framing already in this file from Seb Ospina and the Advance/Gin "naturally talented or very current" pattern above. [XC Mag — The Head Game, featuring Thomas Theurillat and Paul Guschlbauer](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/) (industry/expert) adds a complementary but distinct point: ambition isn't itself the problem — Guschlbauer treats "the wish to achieve certain goals" as the necessary force that drives real progression — the risk is ambition unmatched by honest preparation, not ambition as such. This is a genuine difference in emphasis from sources elsewhere in this knowledge base that treat ambition/aspiration as a red flag in itself; see `disagreements.md`.

## Red flags

- Buying a high-B as an "aspirational" first upgrade rather than because you've demonstrably outgrown your current wing is flagged, in different words, by Advance, Gin, and Seb Ospina's interview.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cloudbase Mayhem Episode 72](https://www.cloudbasemayhem.com/episode-72-greg-hamerton-and-flybubble-gear-choice-bivvy-and-the-road-to-mastery/) (expert, description-level only): says he personally flies gear below his own technical ceiling, and frames matching equipment to demonstrated ability — not aspiration — as the right default. Agrees closely with the Advance/Gin/Ospina pattern above; this is corroboration from an independent (non-manufacturer) expert voice, not a new disagreement.
- Social/peer pressure, named explicitly (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert) names social pressure plainly as a driver of unnecessary risk-taking — his own shorthand covers being watched/filmed, flying in front of a crowd, and comparing yourself to flying partners. This independently corroborates the peer-pressure/ego red flag already recorded from Passion Paragliding (`steps/high-b-to-low-c.md`), from a second, unrelated expert source.

## SIV

Not treated as a gate for this step by any source we found — it's notably absent here even in sources (Flybubble) that start requiring it at the next step up. **One partial exception, added Oct 2026:** Greg Hamerton's "Reducing Your Risk" piece recommends attending a manoeuvres (SIV) clinic before pushing *any* wing's performance envelope, not specifically tied to the high-B step or later — a more general framing than other sources, which only start mentioning SIV from high-B→low-C onward. This doesn't change the brief's instruction to keep SIV optional and non-gating, but it is a genuine point of difference about *when in the ladder* SIV-type training starts being relevant. See new disagreement point in `disagreements.md`.

## Source-quality note

Reasonably well evidenced: sources agree on ~50 hours (with the independence caveat above), and three manufacturer-via-retailer sources independently agree on the "not for beginners/occasional pilots, needs genuine currency" framing. This is one of the stronger-consensus steps in the ladder.
