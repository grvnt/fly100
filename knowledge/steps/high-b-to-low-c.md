# Step: high B → low C

This is arguably the most contested boundary on the entire ladder — the community itself debates whether it's even meaningful (see `disagreements.md`). It's also where SIV first becomes a recurring theme across multiple source types.

## Hours / experience

- [Flybubble — Paragliders: Which Class?](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) (industry): names this tier "Sports (EN C)" and asks for roughly 100 cumulative hours of thermic flying plus some SIV experience.
- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry, different article from the same retailer): gives a lower figure — 75+ hours of thermic flying for EN-C, flown regularly — a modest but real internal inconsistency worth noting (see `disagreements.md`).
- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): higher again — 100+ hours of thermic flying specifically, with "better top speed and ability to cut through turbulence" as the payoff.
- [paraglidingforum.com — When to upgrade to EN B/C?](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=41581) (community, used here only to show rough practitioner consensus, not as a citable criterion): a commonly repeated rule of thumb among posters is roughly 50+ flying hours *per year* (i.e. current, regular flying) before considering EN-C, alongside the more important qualitative test of feeling in control in rough air, not just calm air.
- [Nova — Sector positioning](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/sector/) (manufacturer): frames their entry-level C (Sector) as "a small step" for pilots already comfortable on a benchmark high-B (their Mentor), implying this transition can be gentler than the EN letter change suggests — directly relevant to the high-B/low-C overlap debate.

**Consensus read:** cumulative-hours figures cluster loosely in the 75-100+ hour range across industry sources, with community sentiment adding an annual-currency angle (50+ hours/year) on top. No source gives a hard, well-corroborated single number — treat any figure here as a rough band, not a cutoff.

## Skills

- [xcmag — How to Fly Advanced Paragliders](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/) (industry/expert, Seb Ospina): the EN-C step is where a wing starts transmitting genuinely useful extra information about the air — but only pilots who can already read and interpret that information benefit; pilots who can't will "notice a better glide and some more speed" but miss everything else the wing is telling them. This reframes readiness as an air-reading skill, not just a hours count.
- [USHPA P3/P4 syllabi](https://airaddict.com/p4-certification-requirements/) (official): while written as pilot-rating rather than wing-class criteria, P4's requirement of 250 total flights, 75 hours airtime (25+ of which must be thermal lift), and demonstrated 50%-asymmetric-collapse recovery with directional control is a reasonable official-adjacent proxy for "ready for more demanding gear" at roughly this point in a pilot's development.
- Manufacturer-via-retailer sources (Advance's Sigma, Nova's Sector) describe low-C wings as still "accessible" relative to higher C wings, implying a graduated skill ramp within C itself, not a single skill bar.

## Conditions flown

- Consistent theme: this step marks the first point where sources explicitly expect a pilot to have flown (not just tolerated) genuinely turbulent thermic conditions, not just smooth-day XC.

## Collapse handling

- This is the first step where "precise pilot input required for recovery" (the core EN-C certification language) starts being treated by sources as something a pilot needs to already be good at, rather than something to learn on the new wing. The community consensus captured in the paraglidingforum thread is blunt about the asymmetry of risk: "the penalty for choosing a C when you should be on a B can be rather more severe than the penalty for flying a B when you should have gone for a C" (paraphrased from the thread's recurring theme) — i.e., sources agree err on the side of caution at this specific boundary more than at earlier ones.
- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert) adds a relevant caution here: he's explicit that the EN/AFNOR letter on its own is only a guideline and doesn't tell a pilot how often a given wing actually collapses in practice. This directly corroborates the existing fuzziness already recorded in `disagreements.md` point 3 (whether the sub-class labels are meaningful) — a second, independent expert voice agreeing the letter alone shouldn't be over-trusted at this boundary specifically.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- The community thread on "High End Bs vs. Low EN Cs" shows pilots disagreeing about which actually *feels* calmer in bad air — some find a well-behaved low-C more reassuring in strong turbulence than a twitchy high-B. This argues for the app weighting the pilot's own reported comfort/control in rough air fairly heavily at this step, rather than leaning only on the EN letter.
- Financial/psychological caution flagged repeatedly in community discussion: EN-C wings hold resale value worse than B wings, and "buying a C can be a very expensive mistake" if the pilot isn't ready, while an unnecessary B purchase rarely is — a asymmetric-downside argument that supports a cautious gate at this step.

## Red flags

- Jumping to EN-C mainly because of frustration with a high-B's performance ceiling, without having first been tested in genuinely rough thermic air, is implicitly flagged as a poor reason by both the Seb Ospina interview and the forum consensus.

## SIV

- Flybubble's "Which Class?" article is the first source in the ladder to mention SIV, pairing it with the ~100-hour threshold for EN-C, though it calls it "some SIV experience" rather than a hard requirement.
- Flybubble's "Reducing Risk" article similarly recommends an SIV course "especially" for EN-C-curious pilots, alongside ongoing wingover/spiral/asymmetric practice.
- Per the brief, and consistent with the disagreement captured in `disagreements.md`, SIV should remain optional context here, not a gate — multiple credible sources (Daoust, the forum consensus, USHPA's official syllabi) don't require it at all for reaching this point.
- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert) reinforces Flybubble's recommendation here (unsurprising, given he was Flybubble's own instructor/content lead — see the independence caveat in `steps/mid-b-to-high-b.md` and `disagreements.md` point 6), recommending a manoeuvres clinic before pushing a new wing's performance envelope.

## Source-quality note

Reasonable industry coverage with real but modest internal disagreement on exact hours (75 vs 100 vs 100+). This is the step where sub-class fuzziness (high-B vs low-C overlap) is most clearly documented, so the app should be most cautious about hard EN-letter gating right here.
