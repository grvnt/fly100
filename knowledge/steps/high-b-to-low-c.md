# Step: high B → low C

This is arguably the most contested boundary on the entire ladder — the community itself debates whether it's even meaningful (see `disagreements.md`). It's also where SIV first becomes a recurring theme across multiple source types.

## Hours / experience

- [Flybubble — Paragliders: Which Class?](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) (industry): names this tier "Sports (EN C)" and asks for roughly 100 cumulative hours of thermic flying plus some SIV experience.
- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry, different article from the same retailer): gives a lower figure — 75+ hours of thermic flying for EN-C, flown regularly — a modest but real internal inconsistency worth noting (see `disagreements.md`).
- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): higher again — 100+ hours of thermic flying specifically, with "better top speed and ability to cut through turbulence" as the payoff.
- [paraglidingforum.com — When to upgrade to EN B/C?](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=41581) (community, used here only to show rough practitioner consensus, not as a citable criterion): a commonly repeated rule of thumb among posters is roughly 50+ flying hours *per year* (i.e. current, regular flying) before considering EN-C, alongside the more important qualitative test of feeling in control in rough air, not just calm air.
- [Nova — Sector positioning](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/sector/) (manufacturer): frames their entry-level C (Sector) as "a small step" for pilots already comfortable on a benchmark high-B (their Mentor), implying this transition can be gentler than the EN letter change suggests — directly relevant to the high-B/low-C overlap debate.

**Consensus read:** cumulative-hours figures cluster loosely in the 75-100+ hour range across industry sources, with community sentiment adding an annual-currency angle (50+ hours/year) on top. No source gives a hard, well-corroborated single number — treat any figure here as a rough band, not a cutoff.

## Skills

- [xcmag — How to Fly Advanced Paragliders](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/) (industry/expert, Seb Ospina): the EN-C step is where a wing starts transmitting genuinely useful extra information about the air — but only pilots who can already read and interpret that information benefit; pilots who can't will "notice a better glide and some more speed" but miss everything else the wing is telling them. This reframes readiness as an air-reading skill, not just a hours count.
- [USHPA P3/P4 syllabi](https://airaddict.com/p4-certification-requirements/) (official): while written as pilot-rating rather than wing-class criteria, P4's requirement of 250 total flights, 75 hours airtime (25+ of which must be thermal lift), and demonstrated 50%-asymmetric-collapse recovery with directional control is a reasonable official-adjacent proxy for "ready for more demanding gear" at roughly this point in a pilot's development.
- Manufacturer-via-retailer sources (Advance's Sigma, Nova's Sector) describe low-C wings as still "accessible" relative to higher C wings, implying a graduated skill ramp within C itself, not a single skill bar.
- Speed bar (added Oct 2026): this is the step where sources describe the clearest behavioural step-change in bar use. [A class-by-class speedbar ladder mirrored on GitHub](https://github.com/twpayne/paragliding-articles/blob/master/articles/speed-bar-when-to-use.md) (provenance uncertain, see `sources.md`) states that by EN-C a pilot should expect to glide on half bar routinely, unless in lift or tailwind — a step up from the occasional, between-thermal use expected on EN-B. [XC Mag — Active flying](https://xcmag.com/other/active-flying/) (industry) adds a relevant collapse-risk note for this step specifically: it describes a spectrum from school wings (stable through major collapses) to intermediate wings (self-recovering) to competition-style wings needing active pilot input — EN-C sits in the more demanding part of that spectrum, which is consistent with the EN-C certification language ("precise pilot input required") already covered under Collapse handling below.
- Ground handling (added Oct 2026): no source ties a specific ground-handling test to this exact boundary. [Fly With Greg's ground-handling progression](https://flywithgreg.com/pages/ground-control-training-wing) (expert) is the most relevant general material available — its more advanced exercises (stall-point recognition, depowering, strong-wind launch control) match the kind of active-piloting fluency this step's sources already expect in the air, but no source explicitly states "you should be able to do X on the ground before flying a low-C."
- Full-stall competence as an informal readiness check (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 104 — Mindset, Maximilian Loidl](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0104-mindset-maximilian-loidl/de/) (expert — competitive XC/hike-and-fly pilot, personal account rather than an instructor's standard; treat with the caution his own profile warrants, see `disagreements.md`) describes his own personal rule for deciding he's ready for the next wing: being able to reliably perform full and dynamic stalls, repeatedly, on the current one. He candidly admits he hadn't actually practised this himself until later in his own progression — i.e. he names it as the right test in hindsight, not something he necessarily satisfied at the time. No other source in this knowledge base proposes a stall-competence test specifically at this boundary, so treat as a single, informal, non-corroborated signal rather than an established skill bar.
- [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert, Podz-Glidz podcast, added Oct 2026) is directly relevant here too: he describes EN-C wings as having pitch sensitivity demanding "the lightest touch," and repeats his broader point (see `steps/mid-b-to-high-b.md`) that realistic collapse training becomes much harder to simulate once a pilot is already on a C-class wing — reinforcing that the collapse-recovery skill this step's sources expect should ideally already be well-practised from training on earlier, lower-class gear, not something to pick up on the C-wing itself.

## Conditions flown

- Consistent theme: this step marks the first point where sources explicitly expect a pilot to have flown (not just tolerated) genuinely turbulent thermic conditions, not just smooth-day XC.

## Collapse handling

- This is the first step where "precise pilot input required for recovery" (the core EN-C certification language) starts being treated by sources as something a pilot needs to already be good at, rather than something to learn on the new wing. The community consensus captured in the paraglidingforum thread is blunt about the asymmetry of risk: "the penalty for choosing a C when you should be on a B can be rather more severe than the penalty for flying a B when you should have gone for a C" (paraphrased from the thread's recurring theme) — i.e., sources agree err on the side of caution at this specific boundary more than at earlier ones.
- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert) adds a relevant caution here: he's explicit that the EN/AFNOR letter on its own is only a guideline and doesn't tell a pilot how often a given wing actually collapses in practice. This directly corroborates the existing fuzziness already recorded in `disagreements.md` point 3 (whether the sub-class labels are meaningful) — a second, independent expert voice agreeing the letter alone shouldn't be over-trusted at this boundary specifically.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- The community thread on "High End Bs vs. Low EN Cs" shows pilots disagreeing about which actually *feels* calmer in bad air — some find a well-behaved low-C more reassuring in strong turbulence than a twitchy high-B. This argues for the app weighting the pilot's own reported comfort/control in rough air fairly heavily at this step, rather than leaning only on the EN letter.
- Financial/psychological caution flagged repeatedly in community discussion: EN-C wings hold resale value worse than B wings, and "buying a C can be a very expensive mistake" if the pilot isn't ready, while an unnecessary B purchase rarely is — a asymmetric-downside argument that supports a cautious gate at this step.
- Why they want to upgrade (added Oct 2026): [Passion Paragliding — Which Paraglider? How to Choose a Wing](https://www.passionparagliding.com/how-to-choose-a-paraglider) (industry) is the most pointed source found anywhere in this knowledge base on this exact transition: it states, as the author's own view rather than a cited study, that roughly half of pilots flying EN-C wings shouldn't yet be on them, having upgraded chasing perceived performance rather than a demonstrated need, and names ego ("to validate your identity as a real pilot") and peer pressure explicitly as drivers. It also argues that for most B/C pilots the performance difference within a class is "usually very small," making performance-chasing a particularly weak reason to upgrade at this specific step. This source also gives its own hours figure — 70 hours/year before EN-C — which we've logged in `disagreements.md` alongside the other hours figures for this step.
- [Episode 109 — Mentaltrainerin, Yvonne Dathe](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0109-mentaltrainerin-yvonne-dathe/de/) (expert, Podz-Glidz podcast, German transcript, paraphrased, added Oct 2026) independently corroborates Passion Paragliding's point from a credentialed sports-psychology angle, and gives it a memorable, directly usable name: "Schirmdoping" (wing doping) — upgrading equipment to compensate for a skill gap rather than closing the gap itself. She also frames fear or hesitation before a flight as a protective signal worth listening to rather than overriding, matching Thomas Theurillat's framing already recorded from `xcmag`'s "The Head Game" — two independent credentialed sports psychologists converging on the same point. And she offers a usable reframe for a pilot who hesitates at this step: choosing not to fly (or not to upgrade yet) when honest self-assessed skill doesn't match the conditions or the wing is sound judgement, not something to feel embarrassed about.
- [Episode 114 — Bauchgefühl, Chrigel Maurer](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0114-bauchgefuehl-chrigel-maurer/de/) (expert, Podz-Glidz podcast, added Oct 2026): offers a complementary, slightly different angle — he argues the underlying risk a pilot is exposed to stays roughly similar across skill levels (a beginner is at risk despite modest gear; an expert is at risk because of ambitious goals), and that what actually determines whether that risk stays manageable is trained competence and having a genuine fallback plan, not the nominal ambition of the plan itself. Read alongside Guschlbauer's "ambition is fuel, not a flaw" framing already in `disagreements.md` point 9, this is a second expert voice landing in the same general camp, but via a different argument (competence-and-redundancy, rather than motive-neutrality).

## Red flags

- Jumping to EN-C mainly because of frustration with a high-B's performance ceiling, without having first been tested in genuinely rough thermic air, is implicitly flagged as a poor reason by both the Seb Ospina interview and the forum consensus.
- "Escalation of commitment" as a launch-decision trap (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 35 — Faktor Mensch, Bendicht Erb](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0035-faktor-mensch-bendicht-erb/de/) (expert — flight instructor and certified sports psychologist, survey of 400+ pilots) names a specific cognitive-bias mechanism not found elsewhere in this knowledge base: pilots who've already invested significant effort to get airborne (e.g. a long hike-and-fly approach) rationalising a launch in marginal conditions to avoid "wasting" that effort, rather than assessing the conditions on their own merits. This is a general risk-decision pattern, not specific to the high-B/low-C boundary, but it's a concrete, nameable red flag the app could usefully ask about (e.g. "would you still make this call if you hadn't already hiked/driven/waited to get here?").

## SIV

- Flybubble's "Which Class?" article is the first source in the ladder to mention SIV, pairing it with the ~100-hour threshold for EN-C, though it calls it "some SIV experience" rather than a hard requirement.
- Flybubble's "Reducing Risk" article similarly recommends an SIV course "especially" for EN-C-curious pilots, alongside ongoing wingover/spiral/asymmetric practice.
- Per the brief, and consistent with the disagreement captured in `disagreements.md`, SIV should remain optional context here, not a gate — multiple credible sources (Daoust, the forum consensus, USHPA's official syllabi) don't require it at all for reaching this point.
- [Greg Hamerton — Reducing Your Risk](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) (expert) reinforces Flybubble's recommendation here (unsurprising, given he was Flybubble's own instructor/content lead — see the independence caveat in `steps/mid-b-to-high-b.md` and `disagreements.md` point 6), recommending a manoeuvres clinic before pushing a new wing's performance envelope.
- A genuinely independent, non-Flybubble-linked voice making a related but distinct point (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert — DHV instructor/spokesperson) argues that EN-C-and-above wings resist collapsing enough that realistic collapse-recovery training becomes hard to simulate *on the C-class wing itself* — so the practical implication is that the collapse-recovery training this step's sources want (Flybubble, Malin Lobb) is best done, or at least started, on a lower-class wing beforehand, not deferred until the pilot already owns the C. This is a mechanism-based argument, independent of the Hamerton/Flybubble material, for taking SIV/manoeuvres training seriously even before this exact step — see the update to `disagreements.md` point 8.

## Source-quality note

Reasonable industry coverage with real but modest internal disagreement on exact hours (75 vs 100 vs 100+). This is the step where sub-class fuzziness (high-B vs low-C overlap) is most clearly documented, so the app should be most cautious about hard EN-letter gating right here.
