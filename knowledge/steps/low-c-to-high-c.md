# Step: low C → high C / 2-liner C

This step covers two things sources often conflate: (a) moving from an accessible, 3-line low-C to a more demanding high-C, and (b) moving onto a 2-liner — which may itself be certified C or D depending on the model. We treat 2-liner-ness as its own axis, separate from the C/D letter — see `disagreements.md`.

## Hours / experience

- [richiesadventures.com — Two Liner Progression Tips](https://richiesadventures.com/2019/08/19/two-liner-progression-tips/) (industry/enthusiast, personal-experience piece): recommends 300+ hours on 3-liners before moving to a 2-liner, with roughly the first 50 hours on the 2-liner itself needed just to adapt and meaningfully more (the author suggests 300 hours total on the type) before real competency. This is a notably higher bar than anything found for the C/D letter transition alone, reflecting that 2-liner-ness is treated as its own, stricter hurdle.
- [Cross Country Magazine — First Time Two-Line, featuring Malin Lobb](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) (industry/expert): doesn't give a specific hours number, but is explicit that anyone considering any EN-D, "let alone" a 2-liner, should already be well versed in SIV (see SIV section below).
- [Grands Espaces — EN-C 2-liner paragliders](https://grandsespaces.com/en/en-c-2-liners-paragliders/) (industry, French retailer): positions EN-C 2-liners (e.g. a Photon-class wing) as suited to pilots "very comfortable" on mainstream 3-liner C wings (e.g. a Bonanza or Alpina) — i.e. full competency on a 3-liner C is the implied prerequisite, not just time served.
- [Nova — Mentor/Sector comparison](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/sector/) (manufacturer) and [Skywalk Cayenne 6 positioning, via XC Mag](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/paraglider-reviews/skywalk-cayenne-6-review-en-c/) (manufacturer via press) both describe higher-end C wings (Cayenne 6 specifically) as suited to pilots stepping up from a high-B for their *first* sports wing, or stepping *down* from a high-C/low-D wing for more comfort — showing the low-C/high-C boundary is genuinely two-directional and fuzzy in practice, not a one-way ladder rung.

## Skills

- [Grands Espaces](https://grandsespaces.com/en/en-c-2-liners-paragliders/) (industry): describes 2-liner C wings as requiring more active piloting specifically in turns, compared to the more "intuitive, easy to adjust" turn behaviour of mainstream 3-liner C wings.
- [Cross Country Magazine — First Time Two-Line](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) (industry/expert, Malin Lobb): names specific SIV-trained skills expected before a 2-liner — confident back-flying, managing the energy from a frontal collapse, and efficiently clearing a cravat through a half-spin.
- [Brett Janaway — Thermaling Masterclass (YouTube)](https://youtu.be/G-gqUxq0C6Q) (expert instructor): disagrees specifically on back-flying — he doesn't think it's a necessary skill, even for 2-liners. He puts the real priorities as pitch control and cravat clearing instead. See the SIV disagreement note below and `disagreements.md`.
- [richiesadventures.com](https://richiesadventures.com/2019/08/19/two-liner-progression-tips/) (industry/enthusiast): recommends learning B-line (rear riser) control before adding speed-bar input on a 2-liner, and starting with minimal, conservative inputs rather than "overflying" the wing from day one.
- Speed bar, expanded (added Oct 2026): this step compounds two risks that are each independently documented elsewhere in this knowledge base but not previously connected. [A class-by-class speedbar ladder mirrored on GitHub](https://github.com/twpayne/paragliding-articles/blob/master/articles/speed-bar-when-to-use.md) (provenance uncertain) treats EN-D/competition-style flying as bar-on-constantly, "like changing gears" — and high-C wings, especially 2-liners, sit close to that demand already. At the same time, [Malin Lobb's analysis](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) (already cited below under Collapse handling) describes 2-liners as collapsing "as one block" with higher cravat risk. Put together — our own synthesis, not a single source's claim — this means near-constant bar use is expected on exactly the wings where a collapse is both more likely to happen (lower angle of attack, per the general speed-bar/collapse-risk material in `mid-b-to-high-b.md` and `high-b-to-low-c.md`) and harder to manage cleanly once it does. We did not find a source stating this compounding point explicitly, so treat it as an inference the app should be cautious about overstating as "sourced," even though each half of it individually is well supported.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cloudbase Mayhem Episode 72](https://www.cloudbasemayhem.com/episode-72-greg-hamerton-and-flybubble-gear-choice-bivvy-and-the-road-to-mastery/) (expert, description-level only): flags standard-weight vs lightweight wing construction as its own distinct tradeoff worth understanding at this stage of upgrading, separate from the line-count (2-liner vs 3-liner) question the other sources above focus on — a new angle not otherwise covered in this step.
- Ground handling (added Oct 2026): [paraglidingforum.com — Ground Training/Handling with an old EN-D wing](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=109843) (community, search-summary level only) is directly relevant here even though it's framed around EN-D: experienced posters describe higher-aspect-ratio wings as genuinely harder to kite, to the point of recommending against skipping wing-class steps "even for ground training," and float 150–200 hours per step as a personal rule of thumb — a new, uncorroborated hours figure, logged in `disagreements.md`. No official or manufacturer source addresses ground-handling difficulty specific to 2-liners or high-C wings directly; this community thread is the closest material found.

## Conditions flown

- richiesadventures explicitly recommends starting 2-liner flying in milder conditions, not jumping straight into extreme thermic days, and accepting an extended learning curve.

## Collapse handling

- Malin Lobb's framing (via xcmag) is the most specific in the whole ladder: 2-liners have fewer lines, which gives collapsing fabric more room to tangle, making cravats more likely than on 3-liners; and because 2-liners have more reinforcing rods and span-wise tension, they tend to collapse "as one block" rather than progressively — meaning collapse behaviour on a 2-liner is qualitatively different, not just a scaled-up version of 3-liner behaviour.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cloudbase Mayhem Episode 72](https://www.cloudbasemayhem.com/episode-72-greg-hamerton-and-flybubble-gear-choice-bivvy-and-the-road-to-mastery/) (expert) independently corroborates the general point that 2-liner collapse behaviour is distinct from 3-liner behaviour and worth understanding specifically before upgrading — agreement, not disagreement, with Lobb's more detailed framing above, from a second named expert.
- This is a genuinely distinct risk profile from the high-B→low-C step, which is about *frequency and demand* of active piloting; this step is additionally about a *different failure mode* (block collapses, cravats) that 3-liner experience doesn't fully prepare a pilot for.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- Malin Lobb (via xcmag) makes an interesting point that cuts against assuming this step is uniformly terrifying: a pilot who is already a genuinely high-level flyer may find their first 2-liner flight "remarkably similar" to other advanced wings they've flown — i.e. readiness, not the 2-liner label itself, determines how big this feels.
- richiesadventures frames success at this step as requiring patience, realistic expectations about an extended learning curve, and quality instruction — explicitly warning against treating a 2-liner as a quick performance upgrade.
- Why they want to upgrade (added Oct 2026): no source addresses motivation specifically for the 2-liner/high-C step, but the general pattern recorded elsewhere in this knowledge base applies with extra force here, given how much more unforgiving this step's collapse behaviour is (see above). [XC Mag — The Head Game](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/) (industry/expert) frames honest fear or hesitation before a flight as useful signal rather than something to override — directly relevant to a pilot who feels pulled toward a 2-liner by performance envy (the red flag already named below) but hasn't actually tested themselves against real cravats or block collapses yet.

## Red flags

- Seeking out a 2-liner primarily for the speed/glide numbers without having already experienced (and recovered from) real asymmetric collapses and cravat-type situations on a 3-liner is the clearest red flag pattern across these sources.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cloudbase Mayhem Episode 72](https://www.cloudbasemayhem.com/episode-72-greg-hamerton-and-flybubble-gear-choice-bivvy-and-the-road-to-mastery/) (expert) corroborates this from a different angle: he describes deliberately flying gear below his own technical ceiling and argues pilots generally should match equipment to demonstrated ability rather than aspiration — consistent with, not contradicting, the pattern above.
- The "block collapse" and increased cravat risk described by Malin Lobb should probably translate into the app treating "has this pilot recovered from a real cravat or had cravat-specific training" as more informative at this step than generic collapse-count questions.

## SIV

- This is the step where sources first treat SIV as close to a precondition rather than optional enrichment: Malin Lobb's "well versed in SIV" framing, and the specific skill list (frontal-collapse energy management, cravat recovery via half-spin) assume dedicated SIV training, not just incidental in-flight experience.
- Which specific SIV skill matters most is itself contested: Malin Lobb includes back-flying in the must-have list; Brett Janaway explicitly disagrees that back-flying is necessary, even for 2-liners, and instead names pitch control and cravat clearing as the real priorities. Both treat cravat clearing as important — the disagreement is narrower than it first looks, confined to back-flying specifically. See `disagreements.md`.
- Per the brief, SIV still shouldn't become a hard gate in the app even here — but this is the step where, if the app wants to surface SIV as a *strong recommendation* (optional context, not scored), the evidence best supports that framing. See `disagreements.md`.

## Source-quality note

The 2-liner-specific material (Malin Lobb via xcmag, richiesadventures, Grands Espaces) is reasonably detailed and broadly consistent on skills and collapse behaviour, but hours figures come from only one personal-experience blog (richiesadventures) and aren't independently corroborated — treat the "300 hours" figure as indicative, not authoritative. The low-C/high-C letter boundary itself (as opposed to the 2-liner question) remains thin and bidirectional per the Cayenne 6 example. Greg Hamerton's material (added Oct 2026, description-level only — no transcript was available) is the only genuinely independent expert corroboration we found for the general "2-liner collapse behaviour is distinct" and "match gear to ability" themes; his comments were general podcast remarks, not a dedicated 2-liner-upgrade article, so treat them as supporting colour rather than a primary criterion source for this step.
