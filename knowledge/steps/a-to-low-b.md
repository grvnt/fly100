# Step: A → low B

This is the first and best-evidenced step on the ladder — it's also the step the rubric-designer agent will build first, per the brief.

## Hours / experience

- [Greg Hamerton, via Cross Country Magazine](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) (expert — see `sources.md`, previously logged here without attribution): the first 0–50 hours are best spent building fundamentals on a high-A or low/mid-B wing, i.e. a low-B is explicitly positioned as a reasonable first wing, not just an A.
- [Flybubble — Reducing Risk: Part 2 (Wing)](https://flybubble.com/blog/reducing-risk-wing) (industry): recommends EN-B for recently-licensed pilots once they have at least ~10 hours of airtime; recommends staying on that first B for a long time (150+ cumulative hours) before even thinking about the next step up.
- [406 Paragliding — Worn Out Wing?](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) (industry): frames 0–50 hours as a skills-building phase on A/B-level gear, consistent with the above.
- [USHPA P2 (Novice) syllabus, via AirAddict](https://airaddict.com/p2-certification-requirements/) (official): a formal, numeric example of what "ready to leave pure-beginner gear" looks like even though it's a pilot-rating syllabus rather than a wing-class rule — 35 flights across 7 flying days, 8+ hours of ground school, plus the skills below. It explicitly says the pilot should fly "a canopy recommended by the manufacturer as suitable for Beginner to Intermediate pilots," i.e. it defers to the manufacturer's own class recommendation rather than setting its own EN threshold.
- [Jérôme Daoust — If I had to buy a wing today](https://www.expandingknowledge.com/Jerome/PG/Gear/Best/Wing.htm) (expert): deliberately avoids an hours threshold and instead proposes a skills checklist (below) to decide between A and low-B.

**Consensus read:** sources broadly agree the first tens of hours (order of 0–50) should be on A or low-B gear, and that low-B is an acceptable, even preferable, starting wing for many newly-qualified pilots rather than something you "earn" after A. There is no tight agreement on an exact hours number for leaving A specifically — most sources talk about leaving *beginner gear generally*, not drawing a hard A/low-B line.

## Skills

- Ground handling / kiting: Daoust's checklist asks whether a pilot can reverse-kite for 10+ seconds within the first 8 hours of trying — used as a practical readiness signal rather than a hours count.
- Launch reliability: Daoust also checks for roughly 75% success on forward launches and 65% on reverse launches.
- USHPA P2 skills (official): canopy inflation in a range of wind conditions, airspeed control from near-minimum-sink to fast flight while holding a heading, coordinated 360° turns, landing within 25 feet of a target on 5 attempts, and reserve-deployment/PLF knowledge.
- Currency: Daoust flags flying at least 4 times a month as a precondition for being ready to move beyond pure-A gear; USHPA P2 similarly caps new pilots to narrow wind/turbulence windows (max ~12mph base wind, 15mph gusts, climb rates under 200fpm, bank angle under 30°) until experience grows.
- Speed bar: the clearest guidance found anywhere on the ladder for this step is simply to leave it alone except to escape sinking air — [a class-by-class speedbar ladder mirrored on GitHub](https://github.com/twpayne/paragliding-articles/blob/master/articles/speed-bar-when-to-use.md) (provenance uncertain, see `sources.md`) puts EN-A pilots in this "emergency/sink-escape only" bracket specifically. [Daoust's tips](https://www.expandingknowledge.com/Jerome/PG/Skill/All/J_Tips/English.htm) (expert) reinforce this independently: he recommends near-ground accelerator use for emergencies only, and warns that "stomping" the bar on makes a collapse more likely — engagement should be gradual and progressive from the first time a pilot tries it.
- Ground handling beyond this step: [Flybubble — Improve your ground handling](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/improve-your-ground-handling) (industry) and [Fly With Greg's ground-handling course material](https://flywithgreg.com/pages/ground-control-training-wing) (expert) both frame kiting as a skill with real headroom well past this first step — not something "passed" once and left behind. We've carried the detail about *how far* that headroom goes to the later step files; flagged here so the app doesn't treat Daoust's beginner-level kiting check as the ceiling of what ground handling ever requires.
- Ground handling, concrete exercises (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast): [Episode 37 — Groundhandlix, Urs Aschmann](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0037-groundhandlix-urs-aschmann/de/) (expert, German transcript, paraphrased) gives the most concrete named exercises found anywhere in this knowledge base for this stage: single-side ("asymmetric") inflation in strong wind to build pressure sensitivity before attempting full inflations; a circling technique around the wing's centre, using the rear risers, that deliberately rolls one tip out of the wind window to kill lift before a gust can; controlled stalling via rear-riser pressure so a pilot always has a way to stop the wing; and conscious weight-shift/knee-bend integration, which he says most pilots never learn deliberately. He also gives a concrete minimum-wind figure for flat-ground practice (roughly 3–4 km/h, achieved by raising the A-lines and stepping backward) and a staged site progression (flat ground first, then a gentle ~10° slope, only advancing once the easier stage feels solid) — useful as a soft, descriptive skill ladder rather than a scored test.
- [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik, Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert, German transcript, paraphrased): corroborates Daoust's and Flybubble's emphasis on ground handling at this stage from an independent source (a DHV instructor/spokesperson), and adds that at low-A/low-B level the single most practically useful focus is stall-avoidance technique — specifically a "release and re-apply" (Lösen und Nachziehen) brake rhythm through turns that keeps the wing flying, rather than full-stall training, which he sees as less useful for most pilots at this stage.

## Conditions flown

Sources consistently describe this step as still happening in **gentle, forgiving conditions** — light thermic days, moderate ridge soaring, avoiding rough air by a wide margin. Daoust's checklist explicitly asks whether a pilot can identify and avoid rough air 30+ minutes before it arrives as a gate for moving past pure-A gear.

**Checked specifically, Oct 2026 (owner-prompted gap-check) — strongest thermal/conditions survived without a collapse, as a concrete marker:** not found at this step, or anywhere else in the ladder. The closest wording anywhere in this research is [Cross Country Magazine's "How to Fly XC on an EN A"](https://xcmag.com/news/how-to-fly-xc-on-an-en-a-paraglider/) (industry), which asks whether a pilot has "ridden a leeside thermal that feels like it wants your wing anywhere but in the core" — but this is an open, experiential, yes/no-style question, not a specific collapse-free-survival threshold, and the article doesn't connect it to collapse history at all. Daoust's rough-air-avoidance check (above) and the general "conditions flown" categories already used throughout this knowledge base remain the only sourced material here — both are about avoiding/tolerating conditions in general terms, not about the single strongest thing survived cleanly. Re-check before adding this as a scored question; see `disagreements.md`.

**Checked specifically, Oct 2026 — cross-country distance flown:** not found as a criterion at this step. [USHPA's P2 syllabus](https://airaddict.com/p2-certification-requirements/) (official, re-checked specifically for this) contains no XC-distance figure at all, consistent with this step being about local/ridge flying rather than XC. France's FFVL system (official) draws the same line at a slightly later point: its **Brevet Initial** level (roughly comparable to this step) explicitly only certifies flying "on a known site" in calm conditions, with no distance-flying component — distance flying isn't formally introduced in the French system until the third tier, well past this step. See `steps/high-b-to-low-c.md` for where XC distance does start being used, and `disagreements.md`.

**Checked specifically, Oct 2026 — variety of sites/terrain flown:** not formally required at this step by any official source (USHPA's P2 syllabus has no site-count requirement). But it's worth noting as soft context: [High Adventure's beginner guide](https://www.highadventure.com.au/blogs/news/paragliding-for-beginners-guide) (industry/school) frames exposure to multiple site types (coastal, mountain, training hill) as a deliberate and valuable part of early training, independent of hours — and France's FFVL system draws its first explicit site-related distinction right at the boundary just past this step (Brevet Initial = known site only; Brevet de Pilote = any site), see `steps/low-b-to-mid-b.md`. We don't think this supports a scored site-count question this early, but it's reasonable context for why the app might ask (non-scored) whether a pilot has flown beyond their home site at all.

## Collapse handling

Not heavily emphasised at this step by any source — the A/low-B class boundary is defined mainly by passive recovery behaviour (the wing recovers on its own), so active collapse-recovery skill is treated as something to build *during* this step rather than a prerequisite *for* it.

## Psychological / mindset factors

- [406 Paragliding](https://406paragliding.com/upgrading-your-wing-when-should-you-upgrade-your-wing/) frames readiness partly as emotional: being willing to "start over" on the new wing's learning curve rather than clinging to comfort on the old one.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cloudbase Mayhem Episode 186](https://www.cloudbasemayhem.com/episode-186-checking-the-boxes-with-greg-hamerton/) (expert, description-level only): speaking about the earliest phase of the sport specifically, he frames progression as something that should stay deliberate and gradual from the very start, explicitly warning new pilots against upgrading equipment prematurely or flying into conditions beyond their current level. Agrees in spirit with 406 Paragliding's framing above, though his comments are general mentoring advice rather than aimed at this exact A/low-B boundary.
- Why they want to upgrade (added Oct 2026): no source gave guidance specific to this exact boundary, but two general-readiness frames apply here as much as anywhere later on the ladder. [XC Mag — The Head Game, featuring sports psychologist Thomas Theurillat and X-Alps pilot Paul Guschlbauer](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/) (industry/expert): frames readiness as a challenge-skill balance — moving too fast into a wing that outpaces honest self-assessed skill produces dangerous stress, not growth — and treats goal-driven motivation as legitimate fuel for progression only when it's paired with honest preparation, not a substitute for it. Separately, general sport-psychology research on [task vs ego goal orientation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/psychology/achievement-goal-theory) (academic, not paragliding-specific — see `sources.md`) distinguishes pilots motivated by self-improvement (task-oriented) from pilots motivated mainly by comparison to peers (ego-oriented); we apply this only as general background framing, not a paragliding-specific finding, but it's a reasonable lens for the app's motivation questions at every step, including this first one.

## Red flags

- Daoust's checklist is itself a red-flag list in reverse: a pilot who fails most of his practical checks (reverse kiting, launch success rate, ability to commit to flying regularly, ability to avoid rough air) should stay on A rather than move to low-B.
- No source suggested hard safety red flags specific to this step beyond general under-currency (not flying often enough) and poor ground-handling fundamentals.

## SIV

Not mentioned by any source as relevant to this specific step. This is consistent with the brief's instruction to treat SIV as optional context only, and it means the A→low-B step has no basis in the research for even a light SIV-related question.

## Source-quality note

This step has the best official-adjacent coverage (USHPA's numeric P2 syllabus) of any step in the ladder, plus two independent industry sources roughly agreeing on the 0–50 hour window. Confidence here is comparatively high.
