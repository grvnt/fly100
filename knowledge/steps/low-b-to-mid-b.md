# Step: low B → mid B

This boundary is one of the weakest-evidenced in the whole ladder. Several sources don't acknowledge a "mid B" at all — see `disagreements.md`. Treat everything below as soft signal, not a settled standard.

## Hours / experience

- [paragliding24.ch — low vs mid vs high EN-B](https://paragliding24.ch/en/blogs/blog/en-b-gleitschirm-vergleich-low-vs-mid-vs-high-en-b) (industry, Swiss retailer) is the only source we found that gives explicit numbers for this exact boundary: it frames low-B as roughly 10–30 annual flying hours and mid-B as roughly 30–50 annual flying hours. This is the most specific figure found anywhere in this research, and precisely because of that specificity we're cautious about it — no other source corroborates these particular numbers, and it mixes annual-hours framing with other sources' cumulative-hours framing (see `disagreements.md`).
- [USHPA P3 (Intermediate) syllabus, via AirAddict](https://airaddict.com/p3-certification-requirements/) (official) is a reasonable proxy for this step even though it's a pilot-rating syllabus, not a wing-class rule: 30 flying days, 90 total flights, 20 hours of solo airtime minimum. This is a *much* lower bar than paragliding24.ch's 30-50 annual hours, illustrating how differently sources frame "enough experience" for a similar transition.
- [Greg Hamerton, via Cross Country Magazine — When to move up a class of paraglider](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) (expert — see `sources.md`) doesn't split out a mid-B step explicitly either; his hours bands jump from the 0-50 hour beginner phase to a 50-hour (or ~100-hour ridge-soaring) threshold he associates with "high-B," implicitly treating everything before that as one undifferentiated low/mid-B phase. Worth noting: this is the same named expert whose material underpins the headline hours figures in both the step above and the step below, not three separate voices.

## Skills

- USHPA P3 (official) skills most relevant to this transition: reading wind/thermal/gradient cues, confident launches in a wider range of conditions, linked 180°/360° turns at varied bank angles, and — notably — practising symmetric and asymmetric wing-tip folds for descent control, plus theoretical knowledge of recovery from a 50% asymmetric collapse. This is the first point in the ladder where asymmetric-collapse competence is explicitly named.
- [Jérôme Daoust](https://www.expandingknowledge.com/Jerome/PG/Gear/Best/Wing.htm) (expert) continues to apply the same practical-skills checklist across low/mid/high B rather than hours, and notes that mid-B delivers "intermediate performance" without defining it more precisely than that.

## Conditions flown

No source gave conditions-specific criteria unique to this exact boundary; the general pattern across sources is a gradual widening of acceptable wind strength and thermal activity through the B sub-tiers, continuous with the step below.

## Collapse handling

USHPA P3's explicit introduction of asymmetric (50%) collapse-recovery knowledge is the clearest skill marker we found for this transition — it's reasonable to treat "has practised recognising and correcting an asymmetric collapse" as the defining collapse-handling skill gained during this step, even though no source frames it as a strict gate.

## Source-quality note

This is one of the thinnest steps in the ladder. Only one source (paragliding24.ch) draws this exact boundary with numbers, and we flag it as an outlier rather than a corroborated consensus. The USHPA P3 syllabus is useful as an official-adjacent skills reference but wasn't written with a low-B/mid-B wing distinction in mind at all. Greg Hamerton, a named expert instructor, doesn't draw this boundary with numbers either — he groups low and mid-B together, consistent with the general thinness here rather than adding new resolution. The app should treat low-B→mid-B as a very soft, low-confidence step and avoid implying false precision.
