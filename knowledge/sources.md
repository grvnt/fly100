# Sources

Compiled for the Fly100 Paraglider Upgrade Readiness app. All summaries elsewhere in `knowledge/` are paraphrased in our own words — this file is the link/attribution index. "Accessed" dates are when this research pass was done (October 2026); "published" dates are given where the source itself states one.

Type key: **official** (certifying body / national association / FAI-CIVL), **manufacturer**, **industry** (magazines, retailers, schools, brand-adjacent blogs), **expert** (named instructor/coach/researcher with a track record), **community** (forums — used only to evidence disagreement, never as a criterion source).

## Industry — speed bar and ground handling (added Oct 2026, filling owner-flagged gaps)

- [XC Mag — Hang gliding and Paragliding Techniques: using the speed bar](https://xcmag.com/news/speed-bar-tips/) — industry (magazine, undated legacy technique piece), accessed Oct 2026. Contrasts a heavy-handed vs a smooth pilot to show that gentle, progressive bar engagement buys more height margin before a safe recovery than stomping the bar on fully; is explicit that "almost every glider" is more collapse-prone and reacts more violently when accelerated, and that speed bar should only be used with enough height to recover from a major collapse.
- [XC Mag — Active flying](https://xcmag.com/other/active-flying/) — industry (magazine), accessed Oct 2026. States plainly that a pilot should release the speed bar as soon as a collapse feels possible, and separately notes that school-level wings stay stable even through major collapses, intermediate wings usually self-recover, and competition-level wings need active pilot input to recover — i.e. collapse behaviour on bar is wing-class-dependent, not just a universal risk.
- [Flybubble — How to use your speedbar safely](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/how-to-use-your-speedbar-safely) — industry, accessed Oct 2026. Practical technique piece (positioning in lift bands, hand position, timing, combining with big ears); confirms the general principle that speed bar should be used with excess altitude in smooth air, and flags accelerated low flying near terrain as particularly risky.
- [Flybubble — Speed To Fly Basics](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/speed-to-fly-basics) — industry, accessed Oct 2026. Gives speed-to-fly thresholds by wing category (headwind/sink triggers for using bar) and is explicit that sustained bar pressure reduces angle of attack and therefore collapse margin even though the wing "feels" more solid; recommends a pilot's first few experiments with the accelerator be done with plenty of height in smooth air.
- [GitHub (twpayne/paragliding-articles) — "Speed bar, when to use"](https://github.com/twpayne/paragliding-articles/blob/master/articles/speed-bar-when-to-use.md) — **provenance caveat:** this is a personal archive maintained by a named paragliding pilot (Tom Payne), who states the collection "mostly originally appeared on paraglidingforum.com or Skywings magazine (UK)" (Skywings being the BHPA's own membership magazine), but this specific article carries no byline or publication date in the mirrored copy. Treat as industry/community in origin, not confirmed-official, even though its content (a banded EN-A/B/C/D speedbar-use ladder) reads as consistent, non-fringe guidance. Accessed Oct 2026. Gives the clearest class-by-class behavioural ladder found anywhere: EN-A pilots should only bar out to escape sinking air; EN-B pilots use roughly the first quarter of bar travel about as often as the first half of brake range, with a little bar between thermals; EN-C pilots should expect to glide on half bar routinely unless in lift or tailwind; EN-D/competition pilots treat the bar as a routine control, used continuously, "like changing gears."
- [Flybubble — Improve your ground handling](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/improve-your-ground-handling) — industry, accessed Oct 2026. Explicitly frames ground handling as valuable at every pilot level, not just for beginners, via a ten-exercise progression running from basic reverse-launch control through to recognising, by feel, what a wing looks and feels like just before it collapses — directly linking ground-handling fluency to in-air collapse awareness and active piloting, which is the clearest tie we found between this input and the higher steps on the ladder.
- [Flybubble — BGD SEED (ground handling wing)](https://flybubble.com/bgd-seed) — industry (manufacturer product via retailer), accessed Oct 2026. States ground handling is "never a waste of time, even for professionals," and that deflations, stalls, brake-pressure work and active-piloting drills can all be rehearsed on the ground on a dedicated mini-wing — treated as a training aid for pilots already well beyond the beginner stage, not just novices.
- [Fly With Greg — Ground control training wing](https://flywithgreg.com/pages/ground-control-training-wing) (expert — Greg Hamerton, see his fuller profile below) and [Fly With Greg blog — Get really good at GROUND HANDLING](https://blog.flywithgreg.com/get-really-good-at-ground-handling/) — expert, accessed Oct 2026. Lays out a ten-step ground-handling progression explicitly extending into advanced territory (recognising and catching pitch before a nose collapse, stall-point recognition at thermic launch sites, depowering, kiting uphill, cobra launches in strong wind) — i.e. a named expert instructor treating ground handling as a skill with real headroom well past beginner level, not something "finished" after the first wing.
- [paraglidingforum.com — Ground Training/Handling with an old EN-D wing](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=109843) — community (used only to evidence practitioner sentiment, not as a criterion source), May 2023, accessed Oct 2026 (search-summary level only — direct page fetch returned no content for our tooling). Experienced posters warn that EN-D wings are "a nightmare to ground handle" and not a sensible ground-training tool even for an already-competent pilot, and separately recommend not skipping wing-class steps "even for ground training," floating 150–200 hours per step as a rough personal rule of thumb. This 150–200-hour figure is a new, uncorroborated data point — see `disagreements.md`.
- [USHPA — 2001 Paragliding Accident Summary](https://www.ushpa.org/public/articles/2001-paragliding-accident-summary.aspx) — official (US), accessed Oct 2026. Genuinely useful official data point for the ground-handling gap: found that roughly 64% of accidents happened on the ground or immediately after launch, poor inflation caused about 75% of launch-phase accidents specifically, and — most relevant to the "does this stop mattering" question — accident rates were "essentially equivalent" across P1 through P4 pilot ratings, i.e. official accident data from this period did not show launch/ground-handling problems being something pilots simply age out of as they progress. The report explicitly states it could not draw conclusions about accident rates by wing category, since the population of wings in use wasn't known.

## Industry — motivation and psychological readiness (added Oct 2026, filling owner-flagged gap)

- [XC Mag — The Head Game: Understanding free-flight psychology](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/), by Matt Warren — industry (magazine feature), accessed Oct 2026. Independent of, and unrelated to, the rejected Paraclinic Aotearoa source. Features two named, credentialed voices: **Thomas Theurillat**, a mountain guide and sports psychologist who was Red Bull X-Alps champion Chrigel Maurer's long-time mental-performance supporter across multiple X-Alps races, and **Paul Guschlbauer**, a multi-time Red Bull X-Alps podium finisher and paraglider test pilot. Theurillat frames readiness through a challenge-skill "flow" balance (too much challenge for too little skill produces dangerous stress; too little challenge produces boredom and dropout) and argues that fear or low confidence before a flight is useful signal, not something to override. Guschlbauer frames goal-driven motivation ("the wish to achieve certain goals") as a necessary and legitimate force behind progression, provided it's paired with honest preparation — a notably different emphasis from sources that treat ambition itself as a red flag. See `disagreements.md`.
- [Passion Paragliding — Which Paraglider? How to Choose a Wing](https://www.passionparagliding.com/how-to-choose-a-paraglider) — industry (UK-based school/retailer/adventure operator run by Toby Colombé, a British paragliding team member and world-record holder), accessed Oct 2026. The most direct source we found naming peer pressure and ego explicitly as upgrade motivations: describes ego as "a quiet pressure to prove something, to keep up with others, and to validate your identity as a real pilot," and estimates — as a stated personal view, not a cited study — that roughly half of pilots flying EN-C wings shouldn't yet be on them, having upgraded for perceived performance rather than demonstrated need. Also gives a new hours figure (70 hours/year before considering EN-C) — see `disagreements.md`.
- [ScienceDirect — Achievement Goal Theory (topic overview)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/psychology/achievement-goal-theory) — **academic, general sport-psychology reference, not paragliding-specific.** Accessed Oct 2026. Summarises task/mastery orientation (success defined by self-improvement and effort; associated with seeking out challenge and more resilient coping) versus ego/performance orientation (success defined by outperforming others; associated with avoiding genuine challenge once status feels threatened, and with withdrawal under difficulty). We use this only as independent background grounding for interpreting a pilot's stated upgrade motive — it is not a paragliding study, and when this entry was first written we had not found a paragliding-specific study applying it directly. **Update, Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast pass):** [Episode 109 — Mentaltrainerin, Yvonne Dathe](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0109-mentaltrainerin-yvonne-dathe/de/) (expert — psychology graduate with formal sports-psychology training, ~30 years flying) now gives a genuinely paragliding-specific version of this same distinction, from her own coaching caseload rather than a general literature review — see `steps/low-b-to-mid-b.md` and `steps/high-b-to-low-c.md`. The connection drawn between the two sources is still our own synthesis, not a claim either source makes about the other.
- [PMC — "What factors explain extreme sport participation? A systematic review" (PMC11286560)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11286560/) — **academic, general extreme-sports literature, not paragliding-specific.** Accessed Oct 2026. Reviews 35 studies; relevant findings include that higher self-efficacy/confidence is associated with a willingness to take on more risk, and that "risk seeking and lack of perseverance" were among the strongest predictors of extreme-sports injury across the reviewed literature. Used only as general supporting context, not a paragliding-specific finding.

## Expert — Greg Hamerton (added Oct 2026 review pass, following owner flag that he was missing)

Greg Hamerton: South African-born, UK-based paragliding instructor, qualified since 1992, former Chief Instructor of a Cape Town school, ex-PWC (Paragliding World Cup) competitor, X-Pyr competitor (2016), long-time content lead/instructor at Flybubble before launching his own coaching platform, Fly With Greg, in 2020. Reported ~3,500 hours airtime. Also a published author of paragliding site guides and co-author of an instructional book. Classed as **expert** throughout. Note the overlap-of-interest flagged below: much of his material is published via, or shares house views with, Flybubble, which is already in this knowledge base as an industry source — we treat his pieces as a named expert's voice, but don't double-count them as fully independent corroboration of Flybubble's own articles (see `disagreements.md` point 6).

- [Cross Country Magazine — When to move up a class of paraglider](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) — expert, author confirmed as Greg Hamerton, accessed Oct 2026 (partially paywalled). Gives the 0–50 hours on A/low-mid-B, then 50 hours thermic (or ~100 hours ridge soaring) threshold for high-B, triggered by frustration with sluggish handling/lack of feedback/speed — already used in three step files, now correctly attributed.
- [Cross Country Magazine — Podcast: When should you step up a wing class?](https://xcmag.com/fly-better/paragliding-techniques-paramotoring-skills/podcast-when-should-you-step-up-a-wing-class/) and the same episode hosted on his own site, [Fly With Greg — PODCAST When to upgrade your wing?](https://flywithgreg.com/programs/podcast-upgrading) — expert, ~20-24 minute audio piece, accessed Oct 2026. We could not get a transcript; the page copy frames the central questions as "how do you know it's the right time to move up a class of wing?" and "why do you want to upgrade?" — i.e. a psychological-readiness framing consistent with the brief's emphasis on motive, not just hours. Flagged as description-level only, not fully verified content. **Update, Oct 2026 gap-filling pass:** his written article at [xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) (reachable via a different URL path than the one logged above, same author/content family) confirms this framing in readable text, not just a podcast blurb: it opens on "why do you want to upgrade?", answers it plainly ("to get more performance"), then immediately pivots to "are you ready to upgrade?" — explicitly sequencing motivation before readiness. He also states progression should be "led" by experience (e.g. the frustration-with-current-wing trigger already used in `steps/mid-b-to-high-b.md`) rather than by aspiration. This upgrades our confidence in this specific point from description-level to verified article text.
- [Fly With Greg — Are you ready to UPGRADE? (course page)](https://flywithgreg.com/programs/paraglider-upgrade) — expert (his own commercial course), accessed Oct 2026. Page copy describes a lesson matching airtime to wing-class demands; full lesson content sits behind a paywall and wasn't accessible — flagged as thin/unverified beyond the page blurb.
- [old.fai.org (CIVL safety pages) — Reducing Your Risk, by Greg Hamerton](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton) — expert opinion piece hosted on FAI/CIVL's own safety pages (not an official FAI standard — a named contributor's article), accessed Oct 2026. Sets out a six-factor compounding-risk model (weather, wing selection, site hazards, protective gear, ability development, knowledge) where tightening one factor can offset looseness in another. On wing selection specifically: flying gear beyond your familiarity (especially newly upgraded gear) raises risk; recommends attending a manoeuvres (SIV) clinic before pushing a wing's performance envelope; and is explicitly skeptical that the EN/AFNOR letter alone tells you enough — in his words (paraphrased), the certification class is a guideline that doesn't show how often a given wing actually collapses. Also gives a distinct, concrete "upgrade warning": when you upgrade, deliberately compensate elsewhere — choose more conservative sites, fly less aggressively, and front-load your study of the new wing's specific behaviour, for a period after any class change.
- [Cross Country Magazine — How to: Avoid accidents](https://xcmag.com/fly-better/how-to-avoid-accidents/) — expert, accessed Oct 2026 (paywalled, preview only). Same compounding-risk philosophy as the FAI piece, but the visible preview focuses on launch/landing accident patterns rather than wing-class criteria specifically — only partially relevant to this knowledge base's scope.

## Podcast — Podz-Glidz / Lu-Glidz (Lucian Haas), translated/paraphrased transcripts (added Oct 2026)

[Podz-Glidz transcript archive](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/) is a fan-run, unaffiliated archive of transcripts (195 episodes, ~226 hours of audio) of Lucian Haas's long-running German-language paragliding podcast. Transcripts are primarily German. We fetched individual episode pages and translated/paraphrased the relevant content into our own words throughout the step files; nothing from these transcripts is quoted at length, and German technical terms are only kept in quotes where they're the guest's own coined term for a specific technique, not Lucian Haas's framing. Haas is the interviewer/journalist throughout, never the expert voice himself — sources below are classed by the guest's own credentials, per the owner's instruction. Out of 195 episodes we triaged the full title/guest index and deep-read a deliberately small, high-relevance selection; see the "flagged for a future pass" list at the end of this section for promising titles we did not have time to process.

- [Episode 37 — Groundhandlix, Urs Aschmann](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0037-groundhandlix-urs-aschmann/de/) — expert (ground-handling specialist who runs international courses under his own "Groundhandlix" brand; came to the discipline via rehabilitation after a serious in-flight collapse injury, later trained as a hypnotherapist to complement his teaching), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. Directly fills the ground-handling-skill gap flagged repeatedly elsewhere in this knowledge base (see `disagreements.md` point 11 and the gap notes in several step files): describes ground handling as a lifelong, never-finished skill rather than something "passed" early, and gives concrete named exercises (asymmetric one-side inflation in strong wind to build pressure sensitivity; a circling technique around the canopy's centre using rear risers to deliberately roll an ear out of the wind window before it becomes uncontrollable; controlled stalling via rear-riser pressure; deliberate weight-shift/knee-bend integration; slow-motion repetition to extend reaction time). Also cautions against treating ground handling practice on a brand-new wing at a sharp/demanding site (e.g. dunes) as a substitute for already having solid fundamentals.
- [Episode 131 — Zweileinerei (Two-linering), Melchior Lindenstruth](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0131-zweileinerei-melchior-lindenstruth/de/) — expert (an experienced two-liner pilot, not a credentialed instructor or test pilot — flying two-liners since 2015, and the organiser of a structured survey of roughly 287 EN-C two-liner pilots), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. The most directly relevant single episode we found for the low-C→high-C/2-liner step: gives a specific cautionary case (a pilot with only ~11 hours of experience moving straight to an EN-C two-liner, suffering a severe collapse in turbulent terrain and needing a reserve deployment) and a related rule of thumb that under ~20 hours on the previous category is itself a red flag before attempting a 2-liner. His survey found 60%+ of B→C two-liner transitioners reported the step felt more demanding than expected, and roughly 25% struggled with "ear" (tip) deflation specifically on their new wing. He also describes a collapse-behaviour nuance not found elsewhere in this knowledge base: two-liners can stay noticeably calmer/more stable than 3-liners for longer, but when they do collapse, they tend to do so abruptly and as a single large event rather than giving the small warning flutter a 3-liner typically shows first — i.e. less frequent but less forgiving failure, a different framing from (not a contradiction of) Malin Lobb's "block collapse"/cravat-risk point already in `steps/low-c-to-high-c.md`. Separately flags that most EN-C two-liner buyers don't own the "folding lines" needed to safely practise realistic collapses on their own wing during an SIV course, which he sees as a real training gap manufacturers could close by bundling them. See `disagreements.md`.
- [Episode 112 — Steuertechnik (Steering technique), Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) — expert (DHV — the German hang-gliding/paragliding association — instructor and spokesperson on steering technique, co-designer of safety-class testing protocols, commercial pilot, and a safety trainer who has himself survived a serious frontal-collapse accident), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. Describes active-piloting demands scaling up through the wing-class ladder (from static light-brake flying being actively discouraged even at beginner level, through "contact position" sensing, to fast pendulum-exploiting "impulsive" brake inputs and rear-riser/BC-line steering at mid-B and above). Gives a genuinely new and significant point for this knowledge base: he argues that from EN-C upward, wings become so progressively resistant to collapsing that realistic collapse-recovery training becomes nearly impossible to simulate on the wing itself — meaning the muscle-memory/response training has to happen earlier, on lower-class wings where real collapses can still be safely and realistically induced, not deferred until a pilot is already on a C or D wing. States, as his own comparative view rather than a cited statistic, that a pilot with 100+ hours on a low-B is safer moving onto a high-C than a pilot with only ~20 hours jumping straight there. Also names social pressure explicitly as a driver of unnecessary risk-taking (his own shorthand: cameras, crowds, and watching peers), directly corroborating the ego/peer-pressure red flag already recorded from Passion Paragliding. See `disagreements.md`.
- [Episode 114 — Bauchgefühl (Gut feeling), Chrigel Maurer](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0114-bauchgefuehl-chrigel-maurer/de/) — expert (multiple Red Bull X-Alps champion, widely regarded as the sport's most accomplished XC/adventure pilot), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. Frames decision-making as roughly 90% trained intuition and 10% conscious planning, with the planning (redundant Plan A/B/C options) done before flight specifically so that in-flight decisions are never made from a position of having no fallback. Describes genuine danger as situations that start to feel uncontrollable (e.g. föhn wind compounding unexpectedly with thunderstorm outflow), rather than any fixed numeric threshold. Notably argues that the underlying risk a pilot takes on stays roughly similar across skill levels — a beginner is at risk despite modest equipment, an expert is at risk because of ambitious goals — and that it's trained competence, not the nominal riskiness of the plan, that determines whether that risk stays manageable. This nuances, without contradicting, the ambition-as-fuel-not-flaw framing already recorded from Paul Guschlbauer in `disagreements.md` point 9.
- [Episode 109 — Mentaltrainerin (Mental trainer), Yvonne Dathe](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0109-mentaltrainerin-yvonne-dathe/de/) — expert (psychology graduate with formal sports-psychology/mental-coaching training, ~30 years flying experience, runs a dedicated paragliding mental-coaching practice), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. The clearest paragliding-specific application of the task-vs-ego goal-orientation framework we'd previously only had as a general academic reference (see the ScienceDirect entry above) — she explicitly favours process/attitude-oriented goals (technique, calm, enjoyment) over outcome-oriented ones (podium position, distance vs. peers), and cites her own coaching experience that outcome-fixated pilots show more frustration and poorer judgement. Coins a vivid, directly usable term for a red flag already in this knowledge base: "Schirmdoping" (wing doping) — upgrading equipment to compensate for a skill gap rather than addressing the gap itself. Frames fear as a protective signal to listen to, not override — independently corroborating Thomas Theurillat's framing in `xcmag`'s "The Head Game" from a second credentialed sports psychologist. Recommends anxious pilots take deliberate "mini-steps" outward from their comfort zone rather than large jumps, and frames choosing not to launch when conditions and honest self-assessed skill don't match as sound judgement, not cowardice.
- [Episode 104 — Mindset, Maximilian Loidl](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0104-mindset-maximilian-loidl/de/) — expert (a competitive XC and hike-and-fly racer, 2023 Red Bull X-Alps rookie entrant; not an instructor or coach — read as one assertive, competition-oriented pilot's personal account, not a safety-institutional voice), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. Notable mainly because he's a genuine counter-example to this knowledge base's general "don't skip wing classes" consensus: he deliberately skipped the B class entirely, going from a high-A to an EN-C wing, and later to two further 2-liners — while explicitly saying this aggressive path worked for him personally (citing ten-plus years of other board-sport/balance-sport experience) and isn't something he'd recommend universally. Describes his own informal readiness threshold as being able to reliably perform full and dynamic stalls on a wing before moving to the next one — by his own admission, a skill he hadn't actually practised until his jump to his second 2-liner, i.e. he names the gap in his own progression candidly rather than presenting himself as a model to copy. Also describes a personal burnout episode after flying 250–270 hours in one year, settling on roughly 200 hours/year as his own sustainable ceiling — an anecdote, not a corroborated threshold. See `disagreements.md`.
- [Episode 35 — Faktor Mensch (The human factor), Bendicht Erb](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0035-faktor-mensch-bendicht-erb/de/) — expert (flight instructor at a Swiss paragliding school, certified sports psychologist/mental trainer, author of a postgraduate thesis surveying 400+ pilots on launch-site stress), German transcript, paraphrased, accessed Oct 2026. Contributes a named cognitive-bias mechanism not found elsewhere in this knowledge base: "escalation of commitment" — pilots who've already invested significant effort (e.g. a long hike to launch) rationalising a launch in marginal conditions to avoid "wasting" that effort, rather than assessing the conditions on their own merits. His survey work also found ground-handling deficiency predicts higher launch-site stress specifically, and — a useful nuance — that well-drilled, automatic skills tend to hold up or even improve under spectator pressure, while newer or more complex skills degrade under observation, a general sports-psychology finding he applies directly to paragliding. Shares a personal case of a double frontal collapse at low altitude during a multi-day vol-biv trip, and his recovery path (hypnotherapy, deliberately de-prioritising competitive tracking/XContest, "mini-step" re-exposure) closely parallels Dathe's approach above — independent corroboration from a second credentialed source.

## Official and industry — XC distance, site variety and "conditions survived" as readiness markers (added Oct 2026, owner-prompted gap-check)

The owner asked us to check specifically whether (a) strongest thermal/conditions flown without a collapse, (b) cross-country distance flown, and (c) variety of sites/terrain flown are used anywhere as concrete readiness signals, as opposed to the existing categorical "what kind of air do you mostly fly" and "has it ever collapsed" questions. See `disagreements.md` and the relevant `steps/*.md` files for how this was folded in. Sources below were found or re-checked specifically for this pass.

- [BHPA Advanced Pilot rating — exam revision notes (PDF), hosted by DHPC](https://www.dhpc.org.uk/assets/files/Exam%20Revision%20Material/PresentationAdvancedPilot.pdf) — official (BHPA's own rating-scheme content, reproduced via a regional club's revision material, same pattern as the DHPC EN926 PDF already logged above), accessed Oct 2026. **Extraction caveat:** our tooling could not text-extract this PDF directly; the figures below come from two independent search-engine-indexed summaries of the same document, not a direct read, so treat exact wording as unverified pending a manual check. States that the Advanced Pilot rating requires roughly 100 hours of airtime logged since the Pilot rating, specific cross-country distance flights (individual flights over 10km, 20km and 30km, a declared-goal flight over 10km, and a 20km out-and-return), the FAI Bronze Eagle Badge (15km distance, or 500m height gain, or one hour duration), and completion of a BHPA safety-training (SIV) course. This is a genuinely new, numeric data point for both the XC-distance question and the hours/SIV disagreements already tracked — see `disagreements.md`.
- [Sky Riders — The BHPA Pilot Rating Scheme Explained](https://sky-riders.co.uk/bhpa-pilot-rating-scheme/) — industry (school), re-checked Oct 2026 specifically for XC-distance and site-count wording. Confirms the Pilot rating is the one that unlocks cross-country flying at all, but states no specific distance or site-count figures for any rating — i.e. this third-party explainer doesn't corroborate the specific numbers found in the Advanced Pilot PDF above, which is worth noting given it's already used elsewhere in this knowledge base as a cross-check on BHPA's own material.
- [USHPA P4 Certification Requirements, via AirAddict](https://airaddict.com/p4-certification-requirements/) (already logged above as official/industry) — re-checked Oct 2026 specifically for site-diversity wording we hadn't previously extracted: the P4 (Advanced) rating requires 5 flights at each of 5 different sites in Intermediate-level conditions (3 of them inland), plus at least three 1-hour thermal-lift flights originating from at least 2 different sites, plus having flown a minimum of 5 different canopies. This is the clearest, most concrete official site-variety requirement found anywhere in this research — note it's attached to a pilot rating (P4), not an EN wing letter directly, and the step files below map it to the step where P4 was already being used as a proxy.
- [USHPA P2 Certification Requirements, via AirAddict](https://airaddict.com/p2-certification-requirements/) and [USHPA P3 Certification Requirements, via AirAddict](https://airaddict.com/p3-certification-requirements/) (already logged above) — re-checked Oct 2026 specifically for the same three ideas: neither P2 nor P3 mentions a number of different sites, a cross-country distance, or a thermal-strength-survived metric anywhere in their logged requirements. This is a genuine, useful negative finding — it corroborates, from an official source, that XC distance and site-count don't become formal criteria until later in the pilot-rating ladder (P4), not at the earliest steps.
- [FFVL — Les brevets fédéraux](https://parapente.ffvl.fr/les-brevets-f-d-raux) (already logged above as official) plus two industry explainers reproducing the same official structure, re-checked/found Oct 2026: [École de parapente du Salève — Le brevet de pilote et brevet pilote confirmé](https://www.saleveairlines.com/brevet-de-pilote/) and [Soaring Académie — Tout savoir sur les brevets de pilote FFVL](https://www.soaring.fr/formations/brevets-ffvl/) (both industry/school, French-language, summarised via translation). Together these describe a three-tier French structure where the **Brevet Initial** explicitly certifies flying "on a known site" only, the **Brevet de Pilote** explicitly extends this to "any site" under varied conditions (Soaring Académie's own description frames this stage as pilots deliberately "discovering new sites and varying flight conditions" to build experience), and the **Brevet de Pilote Confirmé** is specifically the level that unlocks and is partly evaluated through genuine distance (cross-country) flying. This is an independent, second-country official structure treating site-variety as a distinct, named milestone separate from hours — corroborating the USHPA P4 site-count finding above, though at an earlier rung of France's own ladder.
- [Scribd — "Brevet pilote confirmé: Déplacement et vol de distance"](https://www.scribd.com/document/622468296/Brevet-pilote-confirme-Deplacement-et-vol-de-distance) — community/document-sharing site hosting what reads as official or school-produced FFVL evaluation criteria, accessed Oct 2026 but **not independently verified**: a secondary, lower-confidence search-summary (not a direct document read, since the full document wasn't accessible to our tooling) suggested the Brevet de Pilote Confirmé requires a minimum of 10 distance flights ("10 vols de distance"). Flag this figure as search-summary level only, same caveat class as the BHPA PDF above — worth a manual re-check before the app relies on it.
- [Joy Paragliding — 10 points when to upgrade your glider for better cross country flying](https://www.joyparagliding.com/10-points-when-to-upgrade-your-glider-for-better-cross-country-flying/) — industry (gear-review/enthusiast blog, same genre as richiesadventures.com and Dust of the Universe already in this knowledge base), accessed Oct 2026 via search-summary only (direct fetch blocked by a DNS/network issue on our end, not a paywall). Directly relevant as a counter-voice on the XC-distance question: its own framing is that there's "no universal answer tied to hours or kilometers" for when to upgrade, and that the glide-ratio/performance difference between adjacent wing categories is often too small for a pilot to actually notice or benefit from. This is a genuine, named pushback against using XC-km as a criterion at all, from a source that is specifically about cross-country flying (not a general upgrade-guidance piece) — see `disagreements.md`.
- [High Adventure — The Ultimate Beginner's Guide To Paragliding](https://www.highadventure.com.au/blogs/news/paragliding-for-beginners-guide) — industry (Australian school), accessed Oct 2026. States plainly that "one of the most valuable parts of learning to paraglide is gaining experience beyond a single flying site," and describes training across multiple site types (coastal headlands, mountains, training hills) and varied launch/landing types as a deliberate part of their own course design. This is a school's own pedagogical framing rather than a wing-class upgrade criterion specifically, and it doesn't tie site variety to any particular hours figure or EN letter — but it's a genuine, named industry voice treating site variety as valuable in its own right, independent of hours or conditions-flown.
- [Cross Country Magazine — How to Fly XC on an EN A](https://xcmag.com/news/how-to-fly-xc-on-an-en-a-paraglider/) — industry, accessed Oct 2026. Poses a short self-check list for a pilot wondering whether they're ready to move off an EN-A: "Have you flown a few cross countries? Done an SIV course? Ridden a leeside thermal that feels like it wants your wing anywhere but in the core? Glided on full bar at cloudbase?" This is the closest wording we found anywhere to the owner's "biggest thermal survived" idea — but it's phrased as an open, qualitative, experiential question ("a leeside thermal that feels like it wants your wing anywhere but in the core"), not a specific, falsifiable "what's the strongest thermal you've flown without a collapse" metric, and it doesn't frame the answer in terms of collapse-free survival specifically. Similarly, "have you flown a few cross countries" is a plain yes/no check, not a distance figure. See `disagreements.md` and `steps/a-to-low-b.md`.
- [Niviuk — Nine key tips to progress in your cross-country flying](https://niviuk.com/en/nine-key-tips-to-progress-in-your-cross-country-flying) — manufacturer, checked Oct 2026 specifically for this gap-check and found not relevant: the piece gives general XC flying technique tips and recommends matching equipment to pilot level in only general terms, with no numeric distance, site-count, or thermal-strength criterion anywhere in it.
- [Flybubble — XC Secrets: How To Fly XC (On A Paraglider)](https://flybubble.com/blog/xc-secrets-how-to-fly-xc-by-paraglider) — industry, checked Oct 2026 specifically for this gap-check.
