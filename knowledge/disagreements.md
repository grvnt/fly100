# Disagreements and how the app should handle them

Every point below is a place where credible sources genuinely conflict, not just a gap in our research. Where we recommend a handling approach, it's meant to inform the rubric-designer agent in step 2 of the build — this file doesn't make scoring decisions itself.

## 1. SIV's value and whether it should gate anything

**The disagreement:**

- Pro-SIV-as-near-mandatory camp: [Flybubble's two articles](https://flybubble.com/blogs/blog/paragliders-which-class) both tie SIV to the EN-C and EN-D steps; [Malin Lobb via Cross Country Magazine](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) treats specific SIV-trained skills (back-flying, frontal-collapse management, cravat recovery) as near-prerequisites for any EN-D or 2-liner wing; [Jocky Sanderson](https://www.jockysanderson.com/) runs SIV courses commercially and is a prominent voice arguing for their value (worth noting as a potential conflict of interest in his framing).
- Within that pro-SIV camp, which specific skill matters is itself contested: [Brett Janaway's Thermaling Masterclass (YouTube)](https://youtu.be/G-gqUxq0C6Q) explicitly disagrees with Lobb on back-flying — he doesn't think it's a necessary skill, even for 2-liners — and instead names pitch control and cravat clearing as the real priorities. Both sources agree cravat clearing matters; the disagreement is narrower than it looks, confined to back-flying specifically.
- Skeptical/mixed camp: community discussion found in our search results describes SIV courses that go badly when a pilot is pushed too hard too early by an inconsistent instructor, sometimes creating fear or distrust rather than confidence; [Jérôme Daoust's](https://www.expandingknowledge.com/Jerome/PG/Gear/Best/Wing.htm) wing-choice methodology doesn't mention SIV at all as a factor in his recommendations; and — most tellingly — even [an official-adjacent competition body](https://www.usparaglidingcompetitions.com/pilot-qualifications/) that requires a formal P4/IPPI-4 pilot rating to compete only "highly recommends," rather than requires, an SIV course, even for its most demanding (CCC-eligible) competition class.
- No official certifying body (EN/CEN, DHV, LTF) mentions SIV at all — it's entirely outside their remit, since they certify wings, not pilots.

**Recommended handling:** Exactly as the brief specifies — SIV should be optional context only, never a hard gate, and should carry light weight at most, even at the D/2-liner/CCC steps where the pro-SIV sources are most emphatic. Credible, non-commercially-interested sources (Daoust, the P4/IPPI-4 competition rules) demonstrate that pilots can reasonably be assessed as ready without having done one. If the app wants to use SIV at all, the safest design is as a small positive signal if completed, never a penalty or cap if not completed, and perhaps as a suggested next step in the "things to work on" output for the C/D/2-liner steps specifically, where the evidence for its usefulness is strongest.

## 2. Hours thresholds, generally

**The disagreement:** figures for the same transition vary meaningfully between sources, and worse, sources mix cumulative-total hours, annual/currency hours, and hours-in-thermic-conditions-specifically without always being clear which they mean. Examples:

- High-B → low-C: Flybubble gives 100 hours cumulative in one article and 75 hours cumulative in another (same retailer, different articles); 406 Paragliding gives 100+ hours; community rule-of-thumb discussion suggests ~50 hours *per year* (currency) as the more important framing.
- Low-B → mid-B → high-B: paragliding24.ch gives a specific annual-hours ladder (10-30 / 30-50 / 50-80 hours/year) found nowhere else, while other sources talk in cumulative terms or avoid hours entirely (Daoust uses skill checks instead).
- High-C → D: three sources converge well on "100+ hours per year, ongoing," but 406 Paragliding adds a cumulative 200+ hour figure on top, which no one else corroborates.
- D → CCC: the only number found (300 hours) is a single secondhand opinion in a personal blog post.
- **New data points, added Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast):** [Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert, DHV instructor) states, as his own comparative judgement rather than a cited study, that a pilot with 100+ hours on a low-B is in a safer position moving onto a high-C than a pilot with only ~20 hours who jumps straight there — broadly consistent in spirit with the ~50-100 hour bands already logged above, but phrased as a relative comparison rather than an absolute threshold. [Melchior Lindenstruth](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0131-zweileinerei-melchior-lindenstruth/de/) (expert, survey of ~287 two-liner pilots) separately treats under ~20 hours on a pilot's *previous* category as itself a red flag specifically before attempting an EN-C two-liner, illustrated with a real case of an ~11-hour pilot suffering a severe collapse and reserve deployment after jumping straight to one. Neither figure is independently corroborated elsewhere — add to the existing pattern of single-source hours data points rather than treating as newly settled.

**Recommended handling:** The app already asks for both total hours and recent/current hours as separate inputs (per the brief) — this maps well onto the cumulative-vs-currency confusion in the sources. Use broad bands (e.g. "tens of hours," "roughly 50-100," "100+ per year ongoing") rather than picking one source's precise figure, and weight recent/currency hours independently from total hours rather than collapsing them into one number. Be explicit in any methodology notes that the exact thresholds are synthesised from several moderately-agreeing industry sources, not drawn from a single authoritative table — because no such table exists.

## 3. Whether low/mid/high B (and low/high C) are real distinctions at all

**The disagreement:** manufacturers themselves are inconsistent. [Niviuk's own guide](https://niviuk.com/en/which-en-b-wing-should-you-choose-based-on-your-level-safety-and-performance) deliberately avoids the low/mid/high B terminology, instead describing a philosophical progression (safety-first → balanced → performance-oriented). Meanwhile [Advance](https://flybubble.com/blog/advance-iota-reviews), [Gin](https://flybubble.com/blog/gin-explorer-paraglider-reviews) and [Nova](https://www.nova.eu/en/gliders/mentor-6/) all clearly do treat "high-B" as a distinct, more demanding tier in their own marketing and retailer-reported positioning. The community itself is split: a [forum thread literally titled "Low and high intra class classification: Is it BS?"](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=112338) captures the skepticism directly, while a separate thread comparing ["High End Bs vs. Low EN Cs"](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=75173) shows pilots disagreeing about which is actually calmer in bad air — i.e. even people who accept the sub-class labels disagree about where the real safety line falls relative to them.

**Recommended handling:** Treat every sub-class label in the ladder as a soft, descriptive band for communicating with the pilot (useful for framing the recommendation in language they recognise), never as a scoring boundary with a hard numeric cutoff. Weight the pilot's self-reported skills, conditions flown, and collapse-recovery comfort more heavily than "which sub-class is the current wing" at the fuzzy boundaries (especially high-B/low-C), since multiple sources show the letter/sub-label doesn't reliably predict how demanding or forgiving a specific wing actually is.

## 4. 2-liner-ness vs the C/D letter

**The disagreement:** 2-liner wings used to be associated only with D/CCC-level wings, but [EN-C-certified 2-liners now exist](https://grandsespaces.com/en/en-c-2-liners-paragliders/) and are explicitly marketed as such. This breaks the assumption that "2-liner" and "D-class" are the same gate. At the same time, [Malin Lobb's analysis](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/first-time-two-line/) of 2-liner collapse behaviour (block-style collapses, higher cravat risk) applies regardless of whether the specific model is certified C or D.

**Recommended handling:** Treat "is this a 2-liner" as its own independent hard-gate trigger, separate from the EN letter. A pilot asking about an EN-C-certified 2-liner should still be asked the stricter 2-liner-specific questions (cravat recovery experience, pitch control, frontal-collapse energy management), not just the standard low-C/high-C questions, even though its official certification letter is "only" C. Given the back-flying disagreement above (point 1), don't gate or score on back-flying specifically — weight cravat-clearing and pitch control instead, where both camps agree.

**Added nuance, Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast):** [Melchior Lindenstruth](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0131-zweileinerei-melchior-lindenstruth/de/) (expert, survey-based) adds a distinct angle that sits alongside Malin Lobb's "block collapse"/cravat-risk framing rather than contradicting it: he describes two-liners as often staying calmer than 3-liners for longer stretches of normal flying, but failing abruptly and with less early warning (no small flutter cue) when they do collapse. Both voices agree the failure mode is qualitatively different on a 2-liner; Lindenstruth's specific emphasis is on the *warning time* being shorter, not just the collapse geometry being different. We treat this as elaboration, not contradiction, but it's worth the app surfacing both framings if it ever explains *why* 2-liners get extra scrutiny, rather than picking one.

## 5. Pilot-rating ladders (BHPA/USHPA/FFVL/SAHPA) vs the EN wing-class ladder

**The disagreement:** these are genuinely two different systems that sources sometimes blur together. USHPA's own P2 syllabus defers to "whatever the manufacturer recommends," rather than specifying an EN letter; BHPA's CP/Pilot/Advanced-Pilot ratings (at least on the public pages we could access) don't state numeric hours or tie explicitly to EN classes at all; SAHPA ties wing-class *categories* (Beginner/Intermediate under a Basic licence; High-Performance/Competition under a Sport licence) to its own licensing tiers, and — interestingly — defaults an unregistered glider to the High-Performance/Competition category if a pilot doesn't actively register a lower class rating, which is a notably conservative-by-default design choice worth considering for the app's own defaults.

**Recommended handling:** Don't ask pilots which national rating they hold as a primary input — most users of a global web app won't have one, and the ratings don't map cleanly onto each other or onto EN A-D anyway. Ask about hours, flights, skills and conditions directly, which is what the brief already specifies. SAHPA's "default to the most conservative category unless proven otherwise" pattern is worth borrowing as a design principle: when evidence is ambiguous, the app's recommendation should default toward "Not yet" or "Nearly ready" rather than "Ready."

## 6. Source-quality outliers worth flagging specifically

- **paragliding24.ch's annual-hours bands** (10-30 / 30-50 / 50-80 / 80+) are the single most precise numeric ladder we found anywhere, attached to every single sub-class boundary with suspicious tidiness. No other source corroborates this exact ladder, and the round, evenly-spaced numbers look more like a marketing-content pattern than a measured consensus. Use with caution; don't treat as more authoritative just because it's more precise.
- **richiesadventures.com's "300 hours before a 2-liner / 300 hours before CCC"** figures are a single enthusiast's personal blog opinion (with the CCC figure itself attributed secondhand to "another pilot" within that post). Treat as indicative colour, not evidence.
- **The two Flybubble articles disagreeing with each other** (75 vs 100 hours for EN-C; and a separate inconsistency about whether the first B should be held for 150+ hours before any further upgrade, vs a ~50-hour threshold for "high-B" named in their other article) show that even a single well-regarded industry source isn't fully internally consistent across its own back-catalogue. This is a useful reminder that "industry consensus" here means rough directional agreement, not precise agreement.
- **The "three independent sources" behind the ~50-hour high-B threshold aren't fully independent (added Oct 2026).** The Cross Country Magazine piece used throughout `steps/a-to-low-b.md`, `steps/low-b-to-mid-b.md` and `steps/mid-b-to-high-b.md` was originally logged as an anonymous industry source but is actually authored by Greg Hamerton, a named expert instructor who was Flybubble's own long-time content lead/instructor before starting his own platform. Since Flybubble is cited separately and repeatedly throughout this knowledge base, several apparent instances of "two or three sources agreeing" on a figure (most visibly the ~50-hour mid-B→high-B consensus) are partly the same house view/voice counted more than once, not fully independent corroboration. This doesn't make the underlying figures wrong, but it means the app's confidence language should be a notch more modest than "three independent sources agree" wherever Hamerton/Flybubble material forms part of that count — 406 Paragliding remains the one genuinely separate voice on the ~50-hour figure.

## 7. What we could not find (true gaps, not disagreements)

- No source — official or otherwise — gives corroborated, CCC-specific pilot-readiness criteria (as opposed to competition-entry licensing requirements). See `knowledge/steps/d-to-ccc.md`.
- BHPA's and DHPC's official PDFs on EN classes could not be text-extracted directly by our tooling; the definitions we used were reconstructed from search-engine-indexed text, not a direct read of the primary document. We recommend a manual review of these two PDFs before treating their exact wording as settled, and especially before quoting anything from them in the app.
- SAHPA's and FFVL's full training manuals (hundreds of pages, partly non-English for FFVL) were only reviewed at a search-summary level, not read in full. Deeper numeric pilot-progression criteria may exist in those manuals that we didn't surface.
- Skywalk's own first-party upgrade guidance (as opposed to third-party retailer/press descriptions of their wings) was not found.
- Greg Hamerton's published material does not give CCC-specific pilot-readiness criteria, despite him being a former competition (PWC) pilot — see `knowledge/steps/d-to-ccc.md`. We checked specifically and found nothing beyond what's already covered for EN-D and 2-liners generally.
- Ground handling at CCC level specifically, and speed-bar technique at CCC level specifically (beyond the FAI's 18cm travel cap, which constrains the wing's design, not the pilot's technique), were checked for and not found — see `knowledge/steps/d-to-ccc.md`.
- No source ties a ground-handling skill test to any boundary between low-B and high-C specifically (as opposed to the beginner step, where Daoust's checklist applies, or EN-D, where community sentiment at least describes the difficulty). This may simply be because ground handling is assumed mastered by this point rather than because it stops mattering — see point 9 below, which gives the strongest evidence either way (official accident data suggesting it doesn't reliably improve with pilot rating).

## 8. How early in the ladder SIV/manoeuvres-clinic experience starts to matter (added Oct 2026)

**The disagreement:** every source already in this knowledge base that mentions SIV at all ties it to the later steps specifically — Flybubble and Malin Lobb first raise it at high-B→low-C, and it becomes close to a precondition only from low-C→high-C/2-liner onward (see point 1 above and `steps/high-b-to-low-c.md`, `steps/low-c-to-high-c.md`). The earlier steps (`a-to-low-b.md`, `low-b-to-mid-b.md`, `mid-b-to-high-b.md`) explicitly note that no source treats SIV as relevant that early.

[Greg Hamerton's "Reducing Your Risk" piece](http://old.fai.org/civl-our-sport/safety/37170-hamerton), hosted on FAI/CIVL's own safety pages, breaks that pattern: he recommends attending a manoeuvres (SIV) clinic before pushing *any* wing's performance envelope, as a general risk-management principle, not tied to a specific EN letter or ladder step. Taken at face value, this would apply even to a pilot moving from a low-B onto a mid- or high-B, well before any other source in this knowledge base considers SIV relevant at all.

**Recommended handling:** this is a genuine difference in framing, not just another data point in the existing SIV debate — it's about *when* SIV-type training starts to matter, not *whether* it matters or *which skill* matters most. Given the brief's instruction to keep SIV optional context and non-gating throughout, we don't think this should change the app's scoring at the earlier steps. But it's reasonable to let it inform the *suggestions* output: a generic "consider a manoeuvres/SIV course before pushing a new wing's limits" nudge could appear as an optional suggestion even at the mid-B→high-B step, where Hamerton is the only source supporting it, rather than reserving that suggestion exclusively for the high-B→low-C step onward as the rest of the evidence base would imply on its own. Keep it as a soft suggestion, not scored, and be aware it rests on a single expert voice at these earlier steps specifically (compare the much broader, multi-source agreement that SIV becomes relevant from high-B→low-C onward).

**Second, independent voice found, Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast):** [Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert — DHV instructor and steering-technique spokesperson; unrelated to Hamerton/Flybubble) makes a related but mechanism-different argument for training collapse recovery earlier: he says that from roughly EN-C upward, wings become too resistant to collapsing for an SIV course to realistically simulate the failure on the wing itself — so the actual muscle-memory/response training has to be built earlier, on lower-class wings, while a realistic collapse can still safely be induced. This strengthens the case for surfacing an SIV/collapse-training suggestion at the earlier steps specifically, since it's now two independent expert voices (Hamerton via a general risk-compounding argument; Winkler via a training-feasibility argument) rather than one, even though they arrive at a similar practical recommendation from different reasoning. Still keep this as a soft, non-scored suggestion per the brief — but we think the earlier-steps suggestion is now better supported than the original note above implies.

## 9. Is ambition/motivation itself a red flag, or only ambition unmatched by preparation? (added Oct 2026)

**The disagreement:** several sources in this knowledge base treat a pilot's stated ambition or aspiration as something close to a warning sign in itself. [Advance and Gin's manufacturer-via-retailer positioning](https://flybubble.com/blog/advance-iota-reviews) warns against buying a high-B "aspirationally"; [Seb Ospina, via xcmag](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/how-to-step-up-flying-advanced-paragliders/), describes his own early jump to high-performance wings as something that set his progression back, offered explicitly as a cautionary tale; and [Passion Paragliding](https://www.passionparagliding.com/how-to-choose-a-paraglider) names ego and peer pressure as common, specifically unhealthy upgrade motives, estimating roughly half of EN-C pilots shouldn't yet be on them.

Set against this, [XC Mag's "The Head Game," featuring sports psychologist Thomas Theurillat and X-Alps podium finisher Paul Guschlbauer](https://xcmag.com/magazine-articles/the-head-game-understanding-free-flight-psychology/), takes a more permissive view of ambition itself: Guschlbauer frames "the wish to achieve certain goals" as the necessary force that drives real progression, not something to be suspicious of — the risk, in this framing, is ambition that outpaces honest preparation, not ambition as a motive in itself. [Greg Hamerton's own upgrade guidance](https://xcmag.com/gear-guide/new-pilots-gear-guide/when-to-move-up-a-glider-class/) sits closer to this second camp too: he states plainly that pilots upgrade "to get more performance" without treating that answer as suspect, then moves straight to assessing readiness — i.e. the motive doesn't need to be noble, it needs to be matched by evidence of readiness.

This is a difference of emphasis more than a flat contradiction — both camps would likely agree that a confident, well-prepared pilot who wants more performance is fine, and an underprepared pilot chasing status is not. But they disagree about where the burden of proof should sit: the first camp treats "why do you want this" as itself diagnostic (an ego-flavoured answer predicts trouble), while the second treats the motivation question as largely neutral and puts the real weight on the separate readiness/preparation questions.

**Recommended handling:** given the brief's instruction that psychological answers should carry real weight, we recommend the app ask about motivation (ambition, peer comparison, XC/competition goals, wanting to keep up with flying partners) as a genuinely scored input — consistent with the first camp — but avoid treating "wants more performance" or "wants to fly with faster friends" alone as a hard penalty, consistent with the second camp's point that this is a normal and even necessary motivator. The more diagnostic combination, supported by both camps, is an ambition-flavoured motive **paired with** thin evidence of readiness elsewhere (low hours, no experience of real collapses, discomfort in rough air) — that combination is where the sources converge on real concern, not the motive in isolation.

## 10. Two new, uncorroborated hours figures (added Oct 2026)

Two additional single-source hours figures turned up during this pass, on top of the ones already catalogued in point 2 and point 6 above:

- [Passion Paragliding](https://www.passionparagliding.com/how-to-choose-a-paraglider) (industry) gives 70 hours/year as its own threshold before considering EN-C — close enough to the existing ~50+ hours/year community rule of thumb already logged under `steps/high-b-to-low-c.md` to be broadly consistent, but still a new, independent figure not corroborated elsewhere, and still annual/currency-framed rather than cumulative, same ambiguity as point 2 describes.
- A [paraglidingforum.com thread on ground-training with an old EN-D wing](https://www.paraglidingforum.com/viewtopic.php?t=109843) (community) floats 150–200 hours *per wing-class step* as a personal rule of thumb, specifically in the context of not skipping steps even for ground-handling practice. This is a different kind of figure from the others in this knowledge base — per-step rather than cumulative-to-a-target-class — and comes from a single forum thread, so should be treated as indicative colour only, same caveat as richiesadventures' 300-hour 2-liner figure in point 6.

**Recommended handling:** no change to the existing guidance in point 2 — keep using broad bands rather than any single figure, and treat both of these as additional, uncorroborated data points rather than new evidence of consensus.

## 11. Does ground-handling competence actually improve with pilot rating? (added Oct 2026)

**The disagreement/tension:** most of this knowledge base's step files assume, implicitly, that ground handling is a skill pilots master early and then carry forward — which is why almost no source discusses it explicitly past the beginner step (see point 7 above). But the one piece of official accident data we found that speaks to this directly cuts somewhat against that assumption: the [USHPA 2001 Paragliding Accident Summary](https://www.ushpa.org/public/articles/2001-paragliding-accident-summary.aspx) (official) found accident rates "essentially equivalent" across P1 through P4 pilot ratings, with launch/ground-phase problems a major and apparently persistent contributor. That's one year of US data from a single national association, not a robust trend line, and the report itself cautions that it couldn't control for wing category — so this shouldn't be overstated. But it's a genuine, if thin, reason to question the "ground handling stops mattering after the beginner step" assumption that most of the industry/expert sources implicitly make by simply not discussing it.

**Recommended handling:** treat ground handling as a skill the app can reasonably ask about at every step, not just the first one, even though specific per-step skill bars don't exist in the sources beyond the beginner step and the general EN-D-is-harder-to-kite community sentiment. Don't invent a numeric ground-handling test for the later steps where none exists — but don't silently drop the question either, since the one piece of official accident data touching on this doesn't support the assumption that it becomes irrelevant.

**Update, Oct 2026 (Podz-Glidz podcast):** [Urs Aschmann](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0037-groundhandlix-urs-aschmann/de/) (expert — a ground-handling specialist instructor) directly supports the "doesn't become irrelevant" side of this question from a different angle than the USHPA accident data: he frames ground handling as a genuinely lifelong, never-finished skill with concrete advanced exercises (e.g. deliberately rolling a wingtip out of the wind window before a gust collapses it) that only make sense for a pilot well past the beginner stage. This is a second, independent reason — alongside the official accident data — to keep a ground-handling question live at every step rather than treating it as "solved" early on.

## 12. Does realistic collapse/SIV training even remain possible on higher-class wings? (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast)

**The disagreement:** every SIV-related source already in this knowledge base (point 1 and point 8 above) frames the question as *whether* and *when* a pilot should get SIV training, implicitly assuming that training is equally available and realistic whatever wing the pilot currently owns. [Simon Winkler](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0112-steuertechnik-simon-winkler/de/) (expert — DHV instructor and steering-technique spokesperson) breaks that assumption: he argues that from roughly EN-C upward, wings are designed to resist collapsing so strongly that an SIV instructor often can't realistically induce a representative collapse on the wing itself — training on such a wing risks becoming, in his framing, an "unrealistic, fear-inducing simulation" rather than genuine skill-building. [Melchior Lindenstruth](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0131-zweileinerei-melchior-lindenstruth/de/) (expert, survey-based) independently raises a related, more practical version of the same problem specifically for EN-C two-liners: most owners don't have the "folding lines" needed to safely rig an artificial collapse on their own wing at all, so even a booked SIV course may not give genuinely representative practice on the wing they actually fly.

Taken together, this means the pro-SIV sources elsewhere in this knowledge base may be *overstating* how much protection "has done an SIV course" buys a pilot once they're already on a C/D-class or two-liner wing specifically — not because SIV lacks value, but because the specific wing in question may be hard or impossible to train realistically on, regardless of how good the course or instructor is.

**Recommended handling:** this doesn't change the brief's instruction to keep SIV optional and non-gating, but it does argue for a more specific, split suggestion rather than one generic "do an SIV course" nudge: (a) encourage collapse-response training *before or during* the lower-class steps, while a wing that can still be realistically collapsed is available to train on (reinforcing the point-8 update above), and (b) if the app does surface an SIV suggestion at the high-C/D/2-liner steps specifically, consider noting that the pilot or course should actively confirm the specific wing can be meaningfully exercised (e.g. folding lines available for a 2-liner), rather than assuming any SIV course on any wing delivers equivalent value.

## 13. Is skipping a wing class ever defensible? (added Oct 2026, Podz-Glidz podcast)

**The disagreement:** every source already in this knowledge base that addresses progression pace at all — Hamerton, Flybubble, 406 Paragliding, the paraglidingforum ground-training thread's 150-200-hours-per-step rule of thumb, Winkler's "100+ hours low-B vs 20 hours straight to C" comparison above — assumes, explicitly or implicitly, that a pilot should move through the ladder one rung at a time, not skip classes. [Maximilian Loidl](https://podz-glidz-transcripts.pages.dev/e/0104-mindset-maximilian-loidl/de/) (expert — competitive XC/hike-and-fly pilot, 2023 Red Bull X-Alps rookie; a single personal account, not an instructor's standard) is a genuine, named counter-example: he deliberately skipped the B class entirely, going from a high-A wing straight to an EN-C, and progressed rapidly through further 2-liners from there. Importantly, he doesn't present this as a universal recommendation — he explicitly credits his own ten-plus years of other balance/board-sport experience (kitesurfing, sailing, skiing) for making the fast path survivable for him, and he's candid that he was emotionally overwhelmed the first time he encountered a 2-liner's full collapse behaviour, and that he hadn't actually practised his own stated readiness test (reliable full/dynamic stalls) until after that experience, not before it.

**Recommended handling:** we don't think this should change the app's default guidance, which should still follow the much broader "don't skip steps" consensus. But Loidl's account is useful as a real, honestly-told example of what the one-rung-at-a-time consensus is protecting against, and it's worth the app's "why does this matter" framing being able to point to a specific lived account rather than only an abstract warning. If the app ever wants to soften its stance for pilots with unusually relevant transferable skill from other action/balance sports, Loidl's own retrospective view — that it worked for him but came with real gaps and real fear he had to work through afterwards, not before — argues for treating transferable-skill claims as a reason for closer questioning (has the pilot actually practised collapse recovery, not just other sports' balance skills), not as a free pass past the ladder.
