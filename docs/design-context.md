# Design context: the fear framework behind the psychological questions

**Internal only. Never surface these terms, or this framing, to a pilot using the app.**

This is a short summary of the model that should shape which psychological questions get asked and how the answers are scored. It is not for display anywhere in the product.

## Two different fears, pointing to opposite advice

A pilot can be hesitant about upgrading for two very different reasons, and they need opposite responses:

- **Fear for the body** — a real sense that their skill doesn't yet match what the new wing demands in bad air. This is a genuine competence signal. It's resolved by more skill and airtime, not by reassurance.
- **Fear for the self** — wanting to upgrade (or hesitating to) because of image, comparison, or status: "everyone I fly with is on a C," "I'll look like I'm behind." This has nothing to do with flying ability. It's resolved by naming what's actually being chased, not by more hours in the air.

Design the psychological questions to tell these apart. "Why do you want to upgrade?" and "what do you expect the new wing to give you?" are there specifically to catch the second kind — if the honest answer is about keeping up rather than about handling, the tool should flag that rather than score it as readiness.

## Raw nerves aren't the problem — the question is what happens next

Physiological arousal (fast heart rate, adrenaline) is neutral on its own. It only becomes a problem when it gets read as a threat and the pilot tightens up and tries to get out of the situation rather than flying through it. So don't ask "are you scared in turbulence" as a yes/no gate — that just measures a universal. Ask what they *do* when it gets rough: do they stay active and keep flying the wing, or does it fall apart. That behavioral response is the signal worth scoring.

## The real mechanism: does skill meet the wing's demands, or fall short

A pilot is ready for a wing when their skill (resources) meets or exceeds what that wing asks of them in bad air (demands). When the demands outrun the resources, that mismatch is where collapses turn into real trouble and reserve throws happen. Every hard-gate input in the brief — collapse frequency and recovery, currency, conditions flown, active piloting — is really asking one question: does this pilot's current resource level already cover the *next* wing's demands, not just the current one.

## The safety guard is not negotiable

Sometimes a pilot's hesitation is simply correct — the gap is real and they're not ready yet. The scoring has to be able to output "not yet" and have that land as a legitimate, successful result of using the tool, never as a failure or something to be argued out of. Don't design any question, score, or suggestion that nudges an accurately hesitant pilot toward upgrading anyway. The job of this app is to get the "not yet" calls right as often as the "ready" calls.

## Tone

Treat fear and hesitation as information, not as a defect. Avoid any wording that casts a "not yet" result, or the pilot's own doubts, as something broken, weak, or irrational that needs fixing. Frame suggestions as "what to work on next," never as "what's wrong with you."

## What to leave out entirely

No mystical, spiritual, or "energy" framing of flow or fear anywhere in this app, even in passing. Keep everything behavioral and concrete — what a pilot actually does under load — not abstract claims about consciousness or states.
